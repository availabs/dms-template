// 01executive summary

The growth is all in the half that has not happened.

New York's headline forecast is +58.4% tonnage and +96.5% real value by 2050, a 1.4% and 2.1% compound annual rate. The number behind it is more awkward: every one of the seven estimated years is below the 2017 benchmark, and 2024 is 3.6% below it — 727.5 million tons against 754.9. Tonnage over 2017–2024 declined at 0.5% a year. Then the series jumps 21.9% between 2024 and 2030, across a six-year span with no column in the file. That discontinuity is not an event; it is a boundary between two methods. The estimated years are modelled forward from the survey; the forecast years come from a different contractor using a commercial macroeconomic model whose inputs were frozen at April 2021, with 2017–2020 treated as “historical forecast” years so that scenario divergence only begins in 2020.

key findings
  • Tons fell at −0.5% a year over the estimated period 2017–2024
  • The 2024→2030 step is 21.9%, across years the file does not contain
  • Value grows 1.5× faster than weight, in constant dollars
  • Exports are the fastest trade type at 2.3% a year
  • The national baseline is 1.1% CAGR; high is 1.3% and low 0.8%

$1.31 trillion or $1.71 trillion for 2024, depending on which value column you read.

The file carries two. value_2024 is $1,306,525M in constant 2017 dollars — the one that is comparable across years. current_value_2024 is $1,713,163M in the dollars of 2024 — the one that matches a contemporaneous economic statistic. The 31% gap between them is inflation, not freight. The current-dollar column exists for 2018–2024 only: there is no current_value_2017 and none for any forecast year, which means a nominal series cannot be extended in either direction. The forecast value figures are real by construction: forecast tonnage multiplied by a forecast constant-dollar price index.

The seven stated assumptions
verbatim from the FHWA webinar · macro data as of April 2021
  1. US economic fundamentals remain solid for the next 30 years
  2. Existing trade policies are assumed to continue
  3. Sustained capital investment in technology, including automation, improves industrial productivity
  4. Retail purchasing shifts further toward e-commerce
  5. Environmental regulation of transportation increases
  6. The energy transition continues away from carbon fuels
  7. Less reliance on petroleum fuel-tax revenues
Two of these — the energy transition and the e-commerce shift — do most of the work in the commodity forecast. Coal falls 88% nationally; basic chemicals grows 3.7% a year.
FHWA's own disclaimer, and it belongs next to any 2050 figure.

“FAF5 forecasts are developed using data and assumptions provided by third parties that do not necessarily represent the views of FHWA or the U.S. Federal Government.” The forecast is a commercial macroeconomic projection mapped onto freight, and the mapping needed two purpose-built crosswalks — 6-digit NAICS to SCTG, and international trade codes to SCTG — whose split fractions are themselves estimates. One published example: petroleum refineries, NAICS 324110, are allocated 60% to gasoline, 30% to fuel oils and 10% to other fossil products. Also worth knowing: 2017–2020 were treated as “historical forecast” years, so scenario divergence only begins in 2020, and the whole update cycle is tied to the survey — every five years, with the economic census.
