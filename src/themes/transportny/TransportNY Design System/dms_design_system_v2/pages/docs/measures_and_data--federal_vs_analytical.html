Federal PM3 and what this site publishes.

Two measure families share the names LOTTR, TTTR and PHED. One is what New York submits to the Federal Highway Administration. The other is the platform's own analytical series. This page tells them apart and says which to cite where. Read it before you quote a reliability figure.

Two families, one set of names

If you are filling in a federal form, use the MAP-21 PM3 pages. Everything else on the platform is the analytical series and is not the compliant figure.

The Moving Ahead for Progress in the 21st Century Act (MAP-21) created the national performance measures. The third performance-measure rule, known as PM3, defines three travel-time measures in 23 CFR 490. They are Level of Travel Time Reliability (LOTTR), Truck Travel Time Reliability (TTTR) and Peak Hour Excessive Delay (PHED). New York submits all three to the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) every year.

The platform also computes an analytical version of the same measures. It uses the same probe feed and the same segments, with deliberately different choices. Questions the federal formula cannot answer can then be answered honestly. The two are separate pipelines that no longer move together. Their numbers will not always agree, and that is by design.

Why the two diverge

The analytical series forked from the federal computation so that it could change. The federal formula follows the rule and stays frozen. The analytical series may redefine a step whenever the measurement shows a better choice. Five differences account for most of the gap you will see.

  • Truck data. The federal TTTR reads the truck stream. The analytical TTTR substitutes an all-vehicle travel time where a bin has no truck value. About 37 % of AM-peak bins carry a substituted value, and removing the substitution moves TTTR by 1.06 %. See TTTR.
  • The delay threshold. The federal excessive-delay threshold is the greater of 20 mph and 60 % of the posted speed limit. The analytical default is 60 % of a free-flow speed anchored to a fixed window, and an unfloored relative variant is published beside it. See the four threshold variants.
  • Roll-up. The federal LOTTR figure is the share of person-miles on reliable segments, weighting each segment by AADT, an occupancy of 1.7 and length. The Macro View shows the per-segment ratio for one period. A statewide percentage and a per-segment ratio are different quantities.
  • What is published beside the value. The analytical series carries a precision band, a minimum-sample bar, coverage percentages and coverage-era tags on every row. The federal submittal carries the value.
  • The network. The analytical series covers every monitored segment, including the expanded network NYSDOT licenses beyond the NHS. The federal figures cover the NHS.

Because the analytical series changes on evidence, a figure from it always carries its methodology date. The federal figure carries only its calendar year.

The two bases differ by 0.2 and 0.1 percentage points on LOTTR and agree on TTTR. The difference comes from how the two datasets are assembled, and PM3 and MAP-21 datasets owns the explanation. Both clear every target. Older handoff copy carried CY 2025 figures that match neither basis. They were a mixed-vintage snapshot that reproduces from no dataset, and they are withdrawn.

Never write a reliability percentage without its calendar year, its system (Interstate or non-Interstate NHS) and its target. "83.2 % reliable" means nothing on its own. "83.2 % of non-Interstate NHS person-miles were reliable in CY 2025, against a 70 % target" is a citable fact.

The measure pages state both forms where both exist. LOTTR, TTTR and PHED each document the analytical computation and then say how the federal roll-up reads the same ratio.

Where this matters

Half of the questions users file about a number are about which family it belongs to. A dashboard value and a submittal value for the same year will differ, and neither is wrong. When you quote one, say which it is, on what basis, and from which page. Which measure should I use? routes each analytical question to its measure. Geography explains why a region's figure is not scored against a regional target.
