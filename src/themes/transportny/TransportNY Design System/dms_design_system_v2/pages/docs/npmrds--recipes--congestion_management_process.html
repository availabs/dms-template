Recipe: a Congestion Management Process.

How an MPO runs the roadway side of a Congestion Management Process (CMP) on this platform: each CMP action, the tool that serves it, the measure to use and what it produces. Rewritten from the 2024 CMP discussion draft for the current tools. Assumes you know your region's CMP network and objectives.

recipe applies to · NPMRDS last reviewed · 2026-09-07 owner · —

The question

"Where is congestion in our region, how bad is it, is it getting worse, and did what we built help?" A CMP is the federally required answer for every Transportation Management Area, an MPO serving more than 200,000 people (23 CFR 450.322). FHWA's guidebook lays it out as eight actions. This platform serves five of them with probe travel-time data on every monitored segment, refreshed as the data arrives, with no field deployment. The other three, objectives, strategy identification and programming, are your process, not a tool's.

The 2024 draft of this discussion promised results from the Mid-Hudson Valley CMP and from the Syracuse Metropolitan Transportation Council's follow-on work. Those results were never published, and this page does not restate them. What it keeps is the draft's one lasting recommendation: rank segments by TED per mile, report them by TED total.

What you need

  • The Macro View and its download, for the regional pass. No sign-in.
  • Route Creation and Reports, for corridors. Sign-in, to save routes and reports.
  • The MAP-21 PM3 pages, for the federal figures your CMP document cites next to its own.
  • A spreadsheet, if you want a composite ranking; see the composite score recipe.
  • Other modes from other sources. The platform holds roadway probe data for all vehicles, trucks and passenger vehicles. Transit, bicycle and pedestrian measures come from elsewhere.
basisThe regional pass is one PM3 year, the MPO chosen under Geography, every monitored segment, the analytical series. Corridor work is the 5-minute observations on saved routes over the dates you set. Federal figures are CY, NHS, statewide.

  1. Open Macro View. Under Geography, pick your MPO under MPOs, or its counties under Counties. Pick the latest Year.The map shows the segments the CMP network can draw on. Segments the probe feed does not cover are not there.
  2. Switch Measure to Coverage · data completeness and read the distribution. Segments with thin data will inflate reliability and deflate delay; decide your screen now (the composite recipe uses 30 % of bins reporting).
  3. Switch to TED with Threshold on Free-flow · fixed reference and Unit on Vehicle Hours. Open Worst 25 segments.The 25 segments with the most vehicle-hours lost in the year, drawn as points on the map. This is a total, so long, busy segments lead.
  4. Build the download with Current filters, adding the LOTTR and TTTR₈₀ columns, the coverage columns and, if you will rank per mile, plan the length join. The composite score recipe takes it from here to a ranked table.
  5. Read TED per mile for the ranking and TED total for the report. Per mile says where a mile of road is worst; total says how much the region loses on that segment. Both go in the document, in separate columns.
  6. For recurrent bottlenecks, rank by PM-peak LOTTR or TTTR₈₀ as well. A segment high on both delay and reliability is congested every day; high on delay alone is busy; high on reliability alone is fragile.
  7. Group the top segments into corridors and save each as a route in Route Creation, tagged with your agency tag.
  8. Open each route in the Snapshot template for an average day, and Year Over Year for the trend. Save the reports and tag them.
  9. For the federal frame, quote the region's row from the MAP-21 PM3 MPO table beside your own figures, naming it as the federal family.
  10. Each year, repeat steps 1 to 5 on the new PM3 year, and run a before/after study on every project that opened.

Reading the result

TED per mile ranks; TED total reports. A three-mile freeway segment and a quarter-mile arterial segment cannot be compared on totals. Divide by length to rank them, then report the total so a reader knows the size of what is lost. Because a floored delay figure on an arterial is about 90 % floor, a ranking that mixes freeways and arterials should use the relative (unfloored) variant; Which measure should I use? says when.

Reliability and delay answer different questions. LOTTR is blind to volume; TED scales with it. Thin data moves them in opposite directions. When they disagree across years, check coverage first.

Trends need the same yardstick. The anchored free-flow reference exists so that a deterioration shows as one. Between years the network map, the coverage era and the AADT vintage all move; the download's README names each for its year. A CMP trend table should carry the era tag per year.

Corridors, not segments, are the unit a strategy acts on. The regional pass finds candidates; the corridor reports say when in the day and which part of the corridor. A signalised arterial reads differently from a freeway: its travel times rise and fall with the signals, so read its averages over months, not days.

Variations

  • A county CMP or a small MPO. Same steps with Counties. Fewer segments clear the coverage screen, so read the row count before you rank.
  • Freight. Rank truck TED (Traffic type · All Trucks) and TTTR₈₀. Truck coverage is thinner and its eras move more.
  • Peak periods only. Use PHED, with its own PM window of 15:00 to 18:59, in person-hours.
  • Many corridors at once. Route Comparison, a draft tool, will put every corridor against every year in one table; until it is released, a report with a Table card and Route Compare gives one row per route. See Compare routes across periods.
  • Earlier plans used the Travel Time Index. TTI is not currently computed. For a comparison with a floating-car era plan, use percentile speed at the 50th and 85th percentiles and say the basis changed.
