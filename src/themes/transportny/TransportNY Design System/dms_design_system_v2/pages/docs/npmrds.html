Start here

One guided task of about 10 minutes: build a route, put it in a report, read the measure. You sign in, open Reports, draw a route from a few segments and save it, create a report, add the route with a date window, add one Line Graph of travel time for the weekday AM peak, and read it. Sign-in is needed from the first step, because both saves write to your account.

NPMRDS runs on its own subdomain, so it may ask you to sign in again when you arrive from another product. Sign in, sessions and accounts explains the arrangement.

Common tasks

  • Find the tool that makes a route. It is New route on the Reports page, not an item in the side navigation.
  • Find a route's recent behaviour with the Snapshot template.
  • Compare a route before and after a change: add the same route twice with two date windows, then a Difference graph. The before/after recipe walks through it.
  • Look at long-term patterns with Year Over Year, Seasonality or Bi-directional.
  • Find the segments with the most delay in a region with the Macro View's worst-25 panel.
  • Export a measure for every segment in a geography, or the data behind a report.
  • Find a report someone in your agency made, by name, tag or owner.
  • Share a report by its address, or print it as a document.
  • Find out why a route lost a segment when you ran it for a later year.
  • Quote a federal figure with its year, system and target.

What the data is good at, and what it is not

NPMRDS is the National Performance Management Research Data Set. The Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) buys probe data, the positions and speeds that vehicles and phones report, and delivers each state its extract. New York's extract covers the National Highway System (NHS) plus the expanded network NYSDOT licenses beyond it. All probes on a segment in one 5-minute epoch are averaged into one travel time, so each segment can have up to 288 values a day. The NPMRDS travel times datasheet has the fields, the span and the feeds.

Good at. It is continuous and statewide, with more than nine years of history and no field deployment. The method is the same year over year, so a before-and-after comparison on the same segments is fair. It has separate all-vehicle, truck and passenger feeds, so freight questions have their own numbers. It replaces a floating-car study wherever coverage exists and the exact start and end points do not matter, with far more observations than any drive could produce. Aggregated to a year it ranks every segment in a region, which is the Congestion Management Process's first step.

Not good at. It measures speed, not volume: delay and cost need an AADT pairing, and the traffic counts lag the travel times by about two years. Coverage is thin on secondary arterials, rural roads and urban streets, so a single day on those roads is more noise than signal; read months, not days. Traffic signals make a segment's averages rise and fall in ways that complicate the reliability and delay measures. The segment map changes every year: codes, lengths and end points move, so a route built on one map can lose or gain segments on another. The TMC network and its vintages explains the mechanism, and Data coverage tells you how much of a segment's day was observed before you trust a pattern.

basisCounts on this page are design-era readings. Home data spine, August 2026: observations from 2017-01-01, 14.47 billion 5-minute records, 52,473 segments on the 2026 map. Report library, August 2026: 869 legacy reports, 32 rebuilt as pages. The live pages recompute their own figures.
