Reliability or delay?

Reliability is a ratio of two travel-time percentiles on one segment. It says how much worse a bad trip is than a typical one, and it is blind to how many vehicles were on the road. Delay is time lost below a threshold speed, multiplied by the vehicles present. It says how much time the network lost, and it scales with traffic volume.

Thin data pushes the two families in opposite directions. It inflates the reliability ratios and deflates the delay measures. So when reliability and delay disagree across years, check Data coverage before concluding anything about the road.

Delay comes from two pipelines. The Macro View's PHED and TED are annual, per segment, on 15-minute bins, with a free-flow reference by default. The TSMO dashboards' excessive delay is monthly, on 5-minute slices, against the posted speed limit, with a median baseline and cause buckets. Never join a figure from one to a figure from the other.

Two axes that move independently

There is no single delay variant that is right for everything, and pretending otherwise would be the real error. Two choices are independent. Which reference the threshold is 60 % of governs whether change over time is measurable. An own-year free-flow reference moves with prevailing traffic, so it cannot register deterioration. A reference anchored to a fixed window can. Whether the 20 mph floor applies governs whether road classes compare to each other. The floor governs 62.7 % of all measured delay and 89.7 % on principal arterials, but 1.3 % on Interstates.

The anchored variant answers the common question. The relative variant answers the cross-class question. The posted-limit variant reproduces the federal formula. The own-year variant exists only to bridge to earlier figures. The four threshold variants lists each with its reference and floor.

Before you quote a delay figure

  • Check the coverage era. Every published row carries an era tag for each stream and a flag when the year crosses a boundary. Use the tag that matches the measure's stream. See Coverage eras and comparability.
  • Check the AADT vintage. Delay scales linearly with traffic volume, and volume comes from an estimated annual average daily traffic (AADT) dataset with its own revision cycle. On a fixed panel of identical segments, directional AADT fell 14.9 % between 2021 and 2022. Traffic does not do that. Read a multi-year delay series per unit of AADT, or pin one vintage. See AADT and its vintage.
  • Check the PM-peak window. LOTTR and TTTR compute their PM peak over 16:00–19:59. PHED computes it over 15:00–18:59 and publishes it under the same label. Do not join the two. See Peak periods and epochs.
  • Know what is missing. 61 % of network mileage is absent from the Macro View's delay family. Those segments have no AADT, no speed limit, or fail the AADT gate. A statewide delay total from the Macro View is a total over the 39 % that qualifies.
basisThe figures on this page are CY 2025, statewide, all vehicles unless the sentence names trucks, on the full monitored network, from the analytical series. The AADT figure is a fixed panel of segments, 2017 to 2022.
