Measures & Methodology.

Every measure this site publishes, what it answers, how it is computed — and the choices behind it, because most of these numbers required a decision that could reasonably have gone another way. Where a measure is fragile, thin, or easy to misread, this page says so rather than leaving you to find out.

// 01

Federal PM3, and what this site publishes

Read this first. Two different measure families share the same names, and telling them apart changes what a number means.

LOTTR, TTTR and PHED are federally defined measures under MAP-21 (23 CFR 490). New York submits them to FHWA every year. This site also computes an analytical version of the same measures — same probe feed, same segments, deliberately different choices — so that questions the federal formula cannot answer can still be answered honestly.

They are separate pipelines that no longer move together. The numbers will not always agree, and that is by design.

// 02

The measure catalogue

Seven measures are published today. Each card answers the same five questions in the same order: what it answers, how it is computed, the choices made, how much to trust it, and where it breaks down. Descriptions are the same text the tool itself shows.

Level of Travel Time Reliability · LOTTR reliability ratio

How much longer a bad trip takes than a typical one. The 80th-percentile travel time divided by the 50th, per segment per peak period.

periods · AM peak · midday · PM peak · weekendstream · all vehiclesa segment is called unreliable at LOTTR ≥ 1.50
LOTTR = TT₈₀ ÷ TT₅₀

per segment × period × year
all-vehicle travel times
15-minute reporting bins
the choices made
  • •The 80/50 percentile pair is the federal definition and is kept unchanged, so the analytical series stays readable next to the submittal.
  • •Nothing is suppressed for thin sample. A segment with few observations still publishes, and carries its own precision columns — flag, never gate. Suppression would bias every roll-up toward well-observed urban segments.
  • •Because the 80th percentile ignores the top 0.1% of trips by construction, LOTTR is the most outlier-robust measure published here. Grouping segments by how heavy their own tail is barely moves it.
how much to trust it

A precision band is published on every row. Measured by down-sampling real segments: the spread of the ratio falls from ±0.079 at 25 observations to ±0.007 at 1,000. The bar for ±0.05 precision is 707 bins, and 85.0% of segments clear it.

known limits
  • •Thin-but-present data pushes LOTTR up: a high percentile of a small sample skews high. A coverage change of the size actually observed moves LOTTR ~3% and the flagged-unreliable population ~23% relative.
  • •Short segments are fragile. 24.3% of segments are under 0.05 mi and are flagged unreliable 7.7× more often (56.0% vs 7.3%) — weight by mileage or VMT before drawing a network conclusion.
  • •Statewide unreliability is 29.1% by segment count, 9.2% by mileage, 17.8% by VMT. Say which one you mean.
  • •Its pmp window is 16:00–19:59 — not the same as PHED’s. See § 05.
Truck Travel Time Reliability · TTTR reliability ratio

The same question for freight, at a stricter percentile. The 95th-percentile truck travel time divided by the 50th, per segment per peak period.

periods · AM · midday · PM · weekend · overnightstream · freight trucks (all-vehicle fallback)federal reporting threshold · TTTR ≥ 1.50
TTTR = TT₉₅ ÷ TT₅₀

freight-truck travel times,
falling back to all-vehicle
where truck data is missing
the choices made
  • •The 95/50 pair is the federal definition, kept unchanged.
  • •Where a bin has no truck data, an all-vehicle travel time is substituted. This is a real choice with a measured cost: about 37% of AM-peak bins carry a substituted value, and removing the substitution moves TTTR by +1.06% (1.8821 → 1.9020 over 400 segments). Defensible, but it means TTTR is not a pure truck measure.
  • •Reading the 95th percentile of a thin stream is what makes this measure expensive. That cost is the entire reason TTTR₀₀ exists alongside it.
how much to trust it

A per-segment TTTR never meets its own precision bar. ±0.05 precision needs 57,832 overnight bins; an overnight year contains 14,600. The bar is unreachable by construction, and only 1.15% of segments clear it in any period. That is the honest signal, not a typo.

known limits
  • •Truck coverage is thin and has changed enormously — truck reporting bins run 8.0% to 23.5% across the nine coverage eras. A year-over-year truck comparison is a coverage comparison unless checked.
  • •For per-segment or corridor work, prefer TTTR₀₀ below. Reserve TTTR for comparison against federal figures and for large aggregates.
  • •Overnight is the federal reporting period for trucks and the thinnest data on the network. Treat single-segment overnight TTTR as indicative only.
Truck Travel Time Reliability, 80th percentile · TTTR₀₀ AVAIL measure ratio

The same ratio as TTTR but taken at the 80th percentile instead of the 95th. Reads lower than TTTR by construction; its value is that it needs a far smaller sample for the same confidence, which matters because truck coverage is thin.

periods · AM · midday · PM · weekend · overnightstream · freight trucks (all-vehicle fallback)not a federal measure
TTTR₈₀ = p80(TT) / p50(TT)

same stream, same bins,
same calculator as TTTR —
read at a shallower percentile
the choices made
  • •This is an AVAIL measure, not a federal one. It exists because the federal one is not estimable per segment on this network.
  • •Nothing else changes — same truck stream, same periods, same calculator. Only the percentile moves, so any difference from TTTR is purely the depth of the tail being read.
  • •Published alongside TTTR rather than replacing it, so the federal figure remains available and the two can be compared directly.
how much to trust it

It reaches the same ±0.05 precision bar at 195 bins against TTTR’s 57,832 — 297× cheaper. 68.8% of segments clear it, against TTTR’s 1.15%.

known limits
  • •Its values are lower than TTTR by construction and must never be presented as a TTTR figure or compared to a federal threshold.
  • •It reads a shallower tail, so it is less sensitive to rare severe events — which is the point, but it means it is not a substitute when the question really is about worst-case freight delay.
Peak Hour Excessive Delay · PHED congestion person-hours

The same, restricted to peak hours and counted per person. Excessive delay is the extra time spent under a speed threshold — 20 mph or 60% of the reference speed, whichever is greater.

periods · AM peak · PM peakcontrols · unit (person / vehicle hours) · per milevolume from directional AADT × hourly factors
threshold = max(0.6 × reference, 20 mph)
delay      = max(0, TT_obs − TT_thr)
PHED       = Σ delay × volume × occupancy

peak hours only
the choices made
  • •The threshold is the federal formula. Which reference speed it is 60% of is a choice, and it changes the number — see § 03 below.
  • •Both floored and unfloored variants are published. The floor is retained by default because without it a street whose achievable free-flow is 13 mph gets a 7.8 mph threshold, and crawling at 9 mph all day would register zero delay.
  • •A per-functional-class floor was considered and rejected on the data: within-class spread exceeds between-class spread, so a per-class constant is nearly as blunt as the global one.
how much to trust it

No precision band is published for the delay family, and that is deliberate: the down-sampling experiment that produced the reliability bands was never run for delay, and a band without a measurement behind it would be a guess. No measured curve → no column → no claim.

known limits
  • •The floor governs most of what you are looking at. 62.7% of all measured delay is computed against a threshold pinned at 20 mph — 89.7% on principal arterials. Removing it moves network delay −41.4% but Interstates only −1.3%.
  • •Never compare delay across functional classes in the floored variants — you would be comparing the floor, not congestion. Use the relative variant. See § 03.
  • •61% of network mileage is absent from the delay family entirely (no AADT, no speed limit, or excluded by the AADT gate). A statewide delay total is a total over the 39% that qualifies.
  • •PHED publishes its PM peak as pmp but computes it over 15:00–18:59, unlike LOTTR/TTTR. See § 05.
Total Excessive Delay · TED congestion vehicle-hours

How many vehicle-hours are lost below the delay threshold speed. Excessive delay is the extra time spent under a speed threshold — 20 mph or 60% of the reference speed, whichever is greater.

period · all hourscontrols · threshold · per mile · AADT sourcePHED is this measure restricted to peak hours and weighted by occupancy
threshold = max(0.6 × reference, 20 mph)
TED = Σ (TT − TT_threshold) × volume

all hours, not just peak
the choices made
  • •TED is the all-hours sibling of PHED and shares its threshold, its reference choice and its floor. Everything in the PHED card applies here.
  • •Reported in vehicle-hours by default, since an all-hours figure has no single defensible occupancy assumption.
how much to trust it

No precision band, for the same reason as PHED. TED is, however, the measure most exposed to outliers of anything published here: grouping segments by their own tail weight moves median TED across a 37× range, against LOTTR’s near-immunity.

known limits
  • •Highly sensitive to extreme single-epoch travel times — but a heavy tail is also what congestion genuinely looks like, so this cannot be screened away with a threshold.
  • •Inherits the AADT vintage problem directly, because delay scales linearly in volume. See § 05 before charting TED over multiple years.
  • •Same 61%-of-mileage absence as PHED.
Percentile speed speed mph

What speed a chosen share of trips beat — the floating-car view. The chosen percentile of observed speed over all travel times.

percentiles · 5th, 20th, 25th, 50th, 75th, 80th, 85th, 95thperiods · all periodsstream · all vehicles
speed = quantile(speed, p)
speed = miles / travel_time × 3600

p ∈ 5, 20, 25, 50, 75, 80, 85, 95
the choices made
  • •Speed is derived, not measured: the feed carries travel time, and speed is segment length divided by it. Segment length therefore enters every speed figure.
  • •Percentiles are taken over observed travel times with no imputation for missing epochs.
how much to trust it

No precision band is published. The percentile depth is the thing to watch: the 5th and 95th read a thin tail and are far less stable than the median on a sparse segment.

known limits
  • •Bad geometry produces impossible speeds. On very short segments a one-second travel-time error becomes a large speed error — and 0.49% of travel times in the feed are under one second.
  • •The feed contains no records below 2 mph — a vendor floor we cannot inspect. Very low speeds are truncated before we see them.
  • •36.2% of records show trucks moving faster than passenger cars, which is physically implausible at scale and is a known property of the feed rather than of the road.
Data coverage data quality percent

Two percentages on the same 0-100 scale. Bins reporting is how much of a bin-based measure’s own input arrived — the sample size its percentiles rest on. Epochs reporting is how much of the raw 5-minute feed arrived, and is lower by construction, since a bin counts as present when any one of its three epochs did.

controls · stream (all vehicles / trucks) · periodpublished per stream per bin, not per measureall-vehicle bins 36.3–51.5% · truck bins 8.0–23.5%
coverage = present / possible

bins reporting   — the sample a
                   percentile rests on
epochs reporting — the raw 5-minute feed
the choices made
  • •Coverage is published as a measure in its own right, not as a footnote, and sits in its own group so it cannot be mistaken for a property of traffic. It describes the input, not the road.
  • •It is published once per stream per bin rather than duplicated onto every measure that reads the same stream.
  • •The bin count is the count of bins where that stream actually had a value — not bins where the feed had any row. Counting feed rows had been inflating the truck figure roughly 2×.
how much to trust it

This is the measure to read first. Coverage is not stationary — there are nine distinct coverage eras between 2017 and 2026 — and it confounds everything else on the map.

known limits
  • •Thin data biases the two families in opposite directions: it inflates the reliability ratios and deflates the delay measures. They will disagree under a coverage change rather than both drifting one way.
  • •88.1% of records rest on four probes or fewer, and trucks are absent from 59.6% of records outright.
  • •Do not compare across coverage eras without saying so. See § 04.

Which delay measure should I use?

Several delay variants are published so that different questions can be answered honestly. That is not an invitation to choose freely — most combinations are wrong for most purposes. Pick from this table and nothing else.

your question use why
Default. How much delay, and is it getting worse?anchored free-flowA fixed reference, so year-over-year change is real rather than partly the yardstick moving. Keeps the floor, so it stays comparable to the federal figure.
Comparing delay across functional classes — where is congestion worst?relative (unfloored)Required, not optional. With the floor in place an arterial figure is ~90% floored and a freeway’s ~3%, so a floored cross-class comparison compares the floor, not congestion.
Delay against the posted speed limitspeed-limit thresholdThe federal formula. Note this measures degradation from a policy number — on roads that cannot reach their posted limit it reports delay during normal operation.
Anything reported to FHWAthe MAP-21 pagesThat pipeline is frozen and is the compliant path. This one is deliberately different. Go there →
Comparing to previously published figuresown-year free-flowTransitional only. Its reference tracks prevailing traffic almost perfectly, so it structurally cannot measure multi-year deterioration. Do not start new analysis on it.
There is no single variant that is right for everything

Pretending otherwise would be the real error. Two axes move independently: which reference is used governs whether change over time is measurable, and whether the floor applies governs whether classes are comparable to each other. The default above answers the common question; the others answer specific ones.

// 04

Coverage is not stationary

The single most common way to misread this data is to treat a change in how much data arrived as a change in traffic.

coverage eras
9

distinct feed regimes between 2017 and 2026. Four of the nine published years blend two eras.

all-vehicle bins reporting
36–52%

range across published years. Not a trend — a property of the feed.

truck bins reporting
8–24%

truck coverage roughly tripled over the period. Any truck trend must control for this.

The two families move in opposite directions

Thinning the probes behind a bin inflates the reliability ratios, while removing reporting bins deflates the delay measures. Same cause, opposite signs. So if reliability and delay appear to disagree across years, check coverage before concluding anything about the road.

The rule: compare within a coverage era, or state that you are crossing one. Every published row carries the coverage columns needed to check.

// 05

Two traps when reading across years and measures

Both of these are known, measured, and easy to walk into.

Trap 1 · a delay trend is partly an AADT revision history affects delay

Delay scales linearly in traffic volume, and volume comes from an estimated AADT dataset with its own revision cycle. On a fixed panel of identical segments, total directional AADT falls 14.9% between 2021 and 2022 — and rose 2.3% in 2020, meaning the series does not register COVID at all. Traffic does not do that; a revision cycle does.

yeardirectional AADT, fixed panelvs 2017
2019272.07M+1.5%
2020274.06M+2.3%
2021273.94M+2.2%
2022227.92M−14.9%

What to do: read a multi-year delay series per unit of AADT, or pin a single AADT vintage. Normalised this way the 2021→2022 step falls from −18.5% to −2.2%. Single-year and cross-sectional comparisons are unaffected — within one year there is one vintage.

The download package flags this automatically when a requested year range straddles the boundary.

Trap 2 · “PM peak” means two different windows affects joins

LOTTR and TTTR compute their PM peak over 16:00–19:59. PHED computes its PM peak over 15:00–18:59 — and publishes it under the same pmp label.

Do not join a LOTTR PM-peak figure to a PHED PM-peak figure and assume the same window. The labels match; the hours do not. This predates the current pipeline and is not corrected, because renaming a published column would break every existing consumer. Coverage is exempt — it publishes both windows separately, so its denominators are unambiguous.

In development

Three measures are declared but not yet computed. They are deliberately kept out of the measure menu — you cannot select one and get an empty map — while still being listed in the macro view’s own measure reference, so the gap is visible rather than silent.

Free-flow speednot yet computed

How fast the road runs when nothing is in the way. The 85th percentile of off-peak observed speed.

Computed internally today as the reference behind the delay family, but not yet published as a measure in its own right.

Emissionsnot yet computed

What the traffic on this segment puts into the air. A speed-binned emission rate applied to the segment’s vehicle-miles travelled.

Blocked on confirming the emission-rate table with NYSDOT. The rate curve is fleet-dependent, so the fleet year it represents will be published alongside every value.

Attributesnot yet computed

Network metadata itself — functional class, AADT, ownership. Coloured straight from a field joined off the network table; no measure controls.

Glossary

TMC
Traffic Message Channel — the road segment this data is reported on. New York has roughly 52,000. Median length is about 0.28 mi, and 24.3% are under 0.05 mi.
Epoch
One 5-minute observation of travel time on one segment. The rawest unit in the feed.
Reporting bin
A 15-minute window — three epochs — averaged before any percentile is taken. This averaging step is the pipeline’s largest outlier suppressor, removing roughly 80% of extreme epochs.
Stream
Which vehicles a travel time describes: all vehicles, passenger vehicles, or freight trucks. The streams arrive independently and on different calendars.
Precision band
The expected spread of a measure at the sample size behind that row, measured by down-sampling real segments rather than assumed from a formula. Advisory — nothing is ever suppressed for being imprecise.
Minimum-n bar
The observation count a measure needs for ±0.05 precision. Published next to the row’s own count so you can make the comparison yourself.
Free-flow reference
The uncongested speed a segment is compared against. Taken as the 15th-percentile travel time, which is equivalent to the conventional 85th percentile of speed.
The floor
The 20 mph minimum on the excessive-delay threshold. It governs 62.7% of all measured delay and almost none of it on freeways.
Coverage era
A period over which the feed’s completeness is stable. There are nine between 2017 and 2026; comparisons within one are safe, comparisons across one need stating.
