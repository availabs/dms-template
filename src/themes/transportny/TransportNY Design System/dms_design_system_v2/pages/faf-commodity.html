// 01executive summary

Three rankings, almost no overlap.

New York's heaviest commodity is natural gas and other fossil products at 147.3 million tons — 19.5% of all state tonnage — and it is thirteenth by value. Its most valuable is miscellaneous manufactured products at $170.8 billion, which is 0.8% of the tonnage. Only two of the forty-two groups appear in both top tens: mixed freight and other foodstuffs. The reason is value density, and it is not a subtle effect: pharmaceuticals move at $66,479 a ton and gravel at $9, a ratio of 7,200 to one. A commodity at the top of that range can pay for air freight and a commodity at the bottom cannot leave its own quarry economically, which is why the value-density column predicts the modal split better than any other field in the file.

key findings
  • Only 2 of 42 groups are in both the tonnage and value top tens
  • The three heaviest groups are 37.6% of tons and 5.9% of value
  • The five dearest groups are 1.2% of tons and 34.4% of value
  • Coal falls 72% by 2050 in New York and 88% nationally
  • Basic chemicals is the fastest-growing group at 2.9% a year

Two of the top-twenty-by-tonnage groups have essentially no recorded value.

Gravel is 71.0 million New York tons at $660 million — $9 a ton. Natural sands is 19.7 million tons at $404 million — $21. Nationally the effect is starker still: in the benchmark composition municipal solid waste is recorded with zero value against 381 million tons, and construction & demolition debris with $4 billion against 569 million tons. These are real movements with real pavement consequences, and every value-weighted screen — benefit-cost, economic impact, dollars per lane-mile — silently drops them.

Why this column predicts the mode

Freight can afford transport in rough proportion to what it is worth. Air carries 0.07% of New York tonnage and 4.1% of its value — and every commodity in its mix is from the top of this table. Pipeline is the mirror: 17.3% of tons, 1.9% of value, and its commodities are all from the bottom. Nothing in the file links these two facts; the ratio does.

the modal split, by commodity →
There are 42 codes, not 43, and there is no code 99.

The codes run 01–41 plus 43. Code 42 does not exist in SCTG as FAF uses it, and neither does an “unknown commodity” bucket: the 2017 survey imputed missing codes with machine learning, so in ORNL's own words FAF5 “does not contain ‘unknown’ commodity shipments in their records.” The string 99 appears in exactly one place in the whole FAF family — the county product's group label sctg3499, which is a group name covering 34 to 43, not a code.

Coal loses 88% of its national tonnage and falls from 7th to 33rd.

790 million domestic tons in 2017, 96 million in 2050 — a −6.2% compound annual rate, the largest single movement anywhere in the forecast. In New York it goes from 2.9 to 0.8 million tons. This is the energy transition arriving as a freight forecast, and it propagates: coal is a rail commodity, which is most of why national single-mode rail tonnage is forecast to shrink at −0.1% a year while every other mode grows. The forecast's macro assumptions were frozen at April 2021.

Three groupings, no published crosswalk

The regional database has 42 codes. The network assignment tables collapse them into 12 groups; the experimental county product collapses them into 5; and the base-year build used a fourth, 9-group Census foreign-trade scheme internally. Joining across these products means reconciling all of them, and no single FAF document sets them side by side. Note the losses: the network's Mixed Freight group merges 40 and 43 — miscellaneous manufactures and mixed freight — which together are 20.8% of New York's freight value.

Caltrans reduces the 42 to 15 for modelling, for the same reason
