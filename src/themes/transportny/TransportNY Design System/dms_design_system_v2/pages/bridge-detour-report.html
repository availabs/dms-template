// approach & method

How the routing is computed.

The method behind every number in the report — how a detour's start and end points are chosen, how the route itself is finalized, and how a "no legal detour" finding is confirmed rather than assumed.

// 01

How this analysis works

The same routing engine behind the live detour tool

Every route in this report is computed by the identical turn-restriction-aware routing engine that powers TransportNY's live detour map — the same road network, the same legal-movement rules, the same distance-and-time model. Nothing here is a simplified or approximate stand-in: this batch analysis simply runs that real engine once for every candidate bridge, in both directions of travel, and records what it finds.

For each bridge, the process has three parts: first, decide where a detour would realistically begin and end near the closure; second, run the real routing search twice — once with the bridge open, once with it closed — and compare the two; third, if the first attempt can't find a legal way through, systematically widen the search until it either finds one or proves there genuinely isn't one. The three sections below walk through each part.

// 02

Deciding where a detour starts and ends

A detour isn't measured from the bridge itself — a driver approaching a closed bridge doesn't stop at the exact structure, they carry on to the nearest real decision point and choose a different way from there. So for every bridge, the analysis first locates the bridge on the road network, then walks outward from each side of the closure — along the same road, in the direction traffic would already be travelling — until it reaches the first genuine intersection: a point where a driver actually has more than one way to go. That intersection becomes the launch point for the detour search on that side.

Why not just start at the bridge?

Starting the search exactly at the closed structure would force every route through whichever single road happens to touch the bridge, even when a driver would obviously peel off earlier at a nearby intersection. Walking out to the first real branch point produces a start and end that match how a detour is actually driven.

A sensible limit on how far it looks

The outward walk is capped — it won't wander for miles down an empty road looking for an intersection. If no branch point turns up within that distance, the search simply uses the furthest point it reached, and that becomes the starting point for the next stage instead.

// 03

Finalizing the route

Once a start and end point are chosen, the routing engine is asked the same question twice for each direction of travel: what's the best legal route between these two points with the network as it normally is, and what's the best legal route with this one bridge removed? Every route respects the same rules a driver actually has to follow — posted turn restrictions, no illegal U-turns, no cutting through a movement that isn't allowed — because the search is exhaustive over the full legal road network, not a shortest-path shortcut that ignores those rules.

step 1
Route with the bridge open

The normal, everyday route between the two points — the baseline every detour is measured against.

step 2
Route with the bridge closed

The same search, but with that one bridge removed from the network — the actual detour a driver would have to take.

step 3
Compare the two

The difference in distance and travel time between the two routes is the reported detour impact — done for both directions of travel independently.

// 04

When the first pair of points doesn't work

Only one bridge is ever closed at a time, so somewhere along that road a legal route to the other side should generally exist — a closed bridge on an isolated dead-end is the exception, not the rule. Rather than reporting "no route" the moment the first attempt fails, the analysis treats that as a sign the starting points were too close to the closure, not as a final answer.

The points move further apart, together

If either direction fails to find a legal route, the analysis pushes that side's starting point further out along the same road it was already following — continuing outward, never doubling back toward the closure — and tries again. Both directions of travel are re-tested together against the updated pair each time, so the two directions always share one coherent, mutually-reachable set of start and end points rather than drifting apart.

A bounded, repeatable process

This widen-and-retry cycle repeats up to several rounds. It stops as soon as both directions succeed against the same pair of points — that pair becomes the final answer for that bridge — or, if a side genuinely runs out of road to expand into, the process stops and the bridge is reported as having no legal detour, rather than forcing a route that doesn't reflect reality.

// 05

Making sure "no legal detour" is a real finding

// verification
A second, independent check for every closure with no legal detour.

A bridge that comes back with no legal detour could mean one of two very different things: the two sides of the closure are genuinely cut off from each other on the road network, or a legal route exists but every path to it happens to be blocked by a turn restriction along the way. Those call for different conclusions, so every no-route result is checked a second way — a plain connectivity check that ignores turn restrictions entirely and asks only "is there any path at all between these points, in either direction?" A closure where that check also fails is a true dead end. A closure where that check succeeds confirms the earlier result was correct for the right reason: a real, drivable route exists in the underlying road network, but every legal way to use it is blocked by a posted movement restriction at some intersection along the way — not a gap in the map data, and not an error in the search.

// 06 · decisions
Why it's built this way.
One consistent identity per bridge

Every bridge is tracked throughout by its own Bridge Identification Number, so results for a given bridge stay traceable and filterable everywhere the analysis references it — the report, the map, and the underlying data all agree on the same identity.

Real routing, not an estimate

Every distance and time figure comes from an actual computed route on the real road network, respecting real turn restrictions — never a straight-line estimate or a simplified stand-in for the true driving distance.

"No legal detour" is reported honestly

Bridges that remain unroutable after the search has been widened as far as it reasonably can go are reported plainly as having no legal detour, rather than the record being hidden or a route being forced that doesn't reflect what a driver could actually do.
