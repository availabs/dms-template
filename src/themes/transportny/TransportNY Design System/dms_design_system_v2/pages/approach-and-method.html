NPMRDS / Bridge detour / Approach & method
// approach & method

How the routing is computed.

The method behind every number in the report: how a detour’s start and end points are chosen, how the route itself is finalized, and how a “no legal detour” finding is confirmed rather than assumed.

// 01

How this analysis works

The same routing engine behind the live detour tool

Every route in this report is computed by the identical turn-restriction-aware routing engine that powers TransportNY’s live detour map: the same road network, the same legal-movement rules, and the same distance-and-time model. Nothing here is a simplified or approximate stand-in for what the live tool would show for the same bridge.

// 02

Deciding where a detour starts and ends

A detour isn’t measured from the bridge itself. A driver approaching a closed bridge doesn’t stop at the exact structure; they carry on to the nearest real decision point and choose a different way from there. So for every bridge, the analysis first locates the bridge on the road network, then walks outward from each side until it reaches the first point that actually offers a real choice of direction.

Why not just start at the bridge?

Starting the search exactly at the closed structure would force every route through whichever single road happens to touch the bridge, even when a driver would obviously peel off earlier at a nearby intersection. Walking out to the first real branch point produces a start and end that match how a detour is actually driven.

A sensible limit on how far it looks

The outward walk is capped, so it won’t wander for miles down an empty road looking for an intersection. If no branch point turns up within that distance, the search simply uses the furthest point it reached, and that becomes the starting point for the next stage instead.

// 03

Finalizing the route

Once a start and end point are chosen, the routing engine is asked the same question twice for each direction of travel: what’s the best legal route between these two points with the network as it normally is, and what’s the best legal route with this one bridge removed? Every route respects the same rules a driver actually has to follow, including posted turn restrictions, not just physical road geometry.

step 1
Route with the bridge open

The normal, everyday route between the two points. This is the baseline every detour is measured against.

step 2
Route with the bridge closed

The same search, but with that one bridge removed from the network. This is the actual detour a driver would have to take.

step 3
Compare the two

The difference in distance and travel time between the two routes is the reported detour impact, calculated for both directions of travel independently.

// 04

When the first pair of points doesn’t work

Only one bridge is ever closed at a time, so somewhere along that road a legal route to the other side should generally exist. A closed bridge on an isolated dead-end is the exception, not the rule. Rather than reporting “no route” the moment the first attempt fails, the analysis treats that as a sign the starting points themselves need to move, not that no detour exists.

The points move further apart, together

If either direction fails to find a legal route, the analysis pushes that side’s starting point further out along the same road it was already following, continuing outward and never doubling back toward the closure, and tries again. Both directions of travel are re-tested together against the updated pair each time, so the two directions always agree on the same pair of points.

A bounded, repeatable process

This widen-and-retry cycle repeats up to several rounds. It stops as soon as both directions succeed against the same pair of points, and that pair becomes the final answer for that bridge. If a side genuinely runs out of road to expand into, the process stops and the bridge is reported as having no legal detour, rather than forcing a route that doesn’t reflect reality.

// 05

Making sure “no legal detour” is a real finding

// verification · a second, independent check for every closure with no legal detour

A bridge that comes back with no legal detour could mean one of two very different things: the two sides of the closure are genuinely cut off from each other on the road network, or a legal route exists but every path to it happens to be blocked by a turn restriction along the way. Those call for different conclusions, so every no-route result is checked a second way, with a plain connectivity check that ignores turn restrictions entirely, confirming whether the two sides are physically connected at all before the finding is reported.

// 06 · decisions

Why it’s built this way.

One consistent identity per bridge

Every bridge is tracked throughout by its own Bridge Identification Number, so results for a given bridge stay traceable and filterable everywhere the analysis references it. The report, the map, and the underlying data all agree on the same identity.

Real routing, not an estimate

Every distance and time figure comes from an actual computed route on the real road network, respecting real turn restrictions. It is never a straight-line estimate or a simplified stand-in for the true driving distance.

“No legal detour” is reported honestly

Bridges that remain unroutable after the search has been widened as far as it reasonably can go are reported plainly as having no legal detour, rather than the record being hidden or a route being forced that doesn’t reflect what a driver could actually do.

// 07 · improvements

What we improved

A review of an earlier version of this analysis flagged three bridges where the reported detour was visibly wrong. Tracing all three back to their root cause led to three separate, general-purpose fixes, and each one was then re-verified against every bridge in the batch, not just the three that were originally flagged.

1. Matching the bridge to the correct road, not the road it crosses

Every bridge point has to be matched to the specific stretch of road it represents in the network graph before anything can be computed. The natural approach, matching to whichever road segment is geographically nearest, fails in one specific and fairly common situation. At an overpass, the bridge deck and the road it crosses sit directly on top of each other in two dimensional coordinates, even though one is at grade and the other is elevated. A nearest match can pick the wrong one entirely, matching a small local road onto the interstate passing beneath it. When that happens, the resulting "detour" tries to route traffic onto a highway with no legal way to get on or off at that location, which is an impossible route, not just an inconvenient one.

The fix

Matching now runs in two passes. The first pass looks only at local class roads, excluding interstates and other limited access highways, and only falls back to an unrestricted, any class match if nothing local is close enough. There is one exception: if the bridge's own official road classification says it genuinely carries an interstate or freeway, the restriction is skipped, so a real interstate bridge isn't incorrectly forced onto a side road. Run again against the full statewide set of candidate bridges, this corrected dozens of similar mismatches with no new errors introduced.

2. Closing the whole physical structure, not just one mapped segment

The digital road network doesn't always represent a bridge as one clean segment. Sometimes the same physical bridge is drawn as several separate one directional pieces, without the matching reverse direction piece that a normal two way road would have. If the analysis only closes the one segment it matched to, the rest of that same physical structure is left open in the network, and the route search happily "detours" straight back across the other, still open half of the very bridge that was supposed to be closed. Depending on exactly how the pieces are arranged, this produced two different failure patterns: a detour reported as having zero impact even though the bridge was a genuine dead end, and a detour whose start and end points collapsed onto essentially the same spot.

The fix, refined over several iterations

The closure logic identifies every one directional segment that belongs to the same physical structure as the matched segment and closes all of them together, not just the one. Getting the boundary of "the same physical structure" right took several corrections in practice. Closing an entire mapped road by name over-reached on long corridors that only partly correspond to the actual bridge. Closing every disconnected segment statewide could wander for miles down an unrelated stretch of the same road. The fix that finally held up bounds the closure at real intersections and caps its overall size, so it reliably captures the bridge itself without spreading into unrelated road segments. This logic also now recognizes a bridge from two independent signals rather than one: a structure explicitly tagged as a bridge in the source map data, or a structure independently confirmed as a bridge through this analysis's own bridge inventory. That way, a real bridge that happens to be missing the first signal is still caught by the second.

3. Reporting "no detour" honestly, distinct from "no impact"

A bridge that is a genuine dead end, where closing it leaves absolutely no other way across, is a fundamentally different finding from a bridge with a real, working, low impact detour. Collapsing the two together, for example by reporting a numeric detour distance of zero for both, makes a true dead end indistinguishable from a bridge that simply has a very good nearby alternate route.

The fix

Every direction of every bridge now gets an explicit outcome: a real computed route with real numbers, or an explicit "no detour found" outcome with no numeric distance at all. There is never a placeholder zero standing in for either case. A "no detour" outcome only gets reported after the search has been widened as far as it reasonably can go, as described in the previous sections, and every such outcome is individually reviewed rather than accepted automatically. Most trace to a genuine, confirmed gap in the road network reachable from that bridge. Some are still open follow up items. Any that turn out to reflect a data or method limitation rather than a real dead end are documented as exactly that, rather than being silently counted alongside the confirmed dead ends.

The output is organized as three related tables, joined by Bridge Identification Number: one row per bridge, one row per direction of travel per bridge, and one row per resolved detour endpoint per bridge.
