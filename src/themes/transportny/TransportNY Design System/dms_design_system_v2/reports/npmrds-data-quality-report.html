// data quality assessment

How much of our reliability signal is real?

The PM3 measures we publish — LOTTR, TTTR, PHED, TED and percentile speeds — are computed from probe-vehicle travel times that are, most of the time, based on four vehicles or fewer. This report quantifies how thin the data is, and shows that the thinner it gets, the worse a segment's reliability appears — which means a large part of what we currently report as unreliability is a measurement artifact, not congestion.

And only 0.13% have 10+ trucks. Every tttr_* and *_truck measure is built on this stream.

The direction of the error is the point.

Sparse data does not make our measures noisy in a way that averages out. It makes segments look less reliable than they are, because LOTTR and TTTR divide a high percentile (80th, 95th) by the median. With four probes in a 15-minute window, the “80th percentile” is effectively the slowest vehicle observed — and the slowest of four is a poor estimate that skews high. So the segments where we have the least information are precisely the ones we most often report as failing.

There is a second, smaller error running the other way: when a period has no data at all, our code publishes LOTTR = 1.00 — perfect reliability. That affects only ~34–160 segments per period, so it is a correctness wart rather than a headline, but it is the wrong default and it would matter a great deal more if we ever reported monthly instead of annually.

A third of our segments carry 1.5% of the mileage — and most of the bad news.

TMC segments vary from a few hundred feet to many miles. On a very short segment the travel time is a handful of seconds, so where a vehicle happened to catch the signal cycle dominates the measurement. Virginia found 60% of NPMRDS-flagged unreliable segments were under a quarter mile. In New York the effect is stronger.

Perfectly monotonic in both directions: as segments get shorter, mean LOTTR rises and the flagged rate rises. The sub-0.1-mile band alone holds 17,394 segments — a third of the network by count, 1.5% by mileage — and more than half of them are reported as unreliable. Any statewide statistic computed per-segment rather than per-mile is largely a statement about these segments.

// 05

“How much of the network is unreliable?” has three defensible answers.

Because short segments are both over-represented in count and over-flagged, the headline number depends heavily on a choice most readers never see being made.

weighted by segment count
29.1%

Every segment counts once, so a 300-foot ramp counts as much as a 10-mile rural corridor. Mean AM LOTTR 1.321.

weighted by mileage
9.2%

Each mile of road counts once. Mean AM LOTTR 1.200.

weighted by VMT
17.8%

Miles × directional AADT — closest to “what travellers experience”. Mean AM LOTTR 1.183.

A 3.2× spread from the weighting choice alone. VMT weighting sitting between the other two is informative: the flagged short segments are not merely numerous, some of them do carry real traffic. But reporting the 29.1% figure without saying it is segment-counted would substantially overstate the problem.

This is an assumption-light error signal that needs no ground truth: on the same segment in the same 5 minutes, trucks are reported faster than cars in over a third of records. NCHRP found 25–35% in 2014 (NCHRP 854); we measure 36.2% in 2025, so this has not improved.

Prevalence is not leverage — and our pipeline skips the step everyone recommends

A 0.49% contamination rate sounds ignorable, and for a mean it is — one group found outliers had “negligible effect on summary statistics”. But that is a statement about averages. For an 80th percentile computed from four observations, one impossible value can move the answer entirely. The published work is unambiguous about the fix: convert travel time to speed, screen it (<2 mph, <5 mph, >75 mph, class-specific caps), then aggregate. We aggregate raw travel times in ClickHouse and never form a speed at all outside the percentile-speed metric — so none of those screens can fire.

// 07

What this report does not establish.

We have no ground truth.

The sample-size gradient is strong evidence that sparse segments are biased, but it does not prove the well-observed value is correct. Establishing true error needs a second source (Bluetooth re-identification, floating-car runs, or a vendor comparison) that we do not hold.

Correlation, not decomposition.

Short segments have both fewer probes and more quantization error, and rural segments have both fewer probes and genuinely different traffic. We have not separated “sparsity bias” from “short-segment measurement error” from “real rural variability”. The down-sampling experiments in the research plan are designed to do that.

PHED and TED are unexamined.

Eight of our eleven metrics are the PHED/TED family. They embed a 15th-percentile free-flow threshold — itself a small-sample quantile — and multiply through AADT and vehicle occupancy. No published work covers their sensitivity, and this report does not either.

A precision floor we cannot remove.

The federal rule requires LOTTR to hundredths. ClickHouse's parallel float averaging is order-dependent, and we measured 13 values out of 4.95 million flipping by 0.01 between two identical runs. Small, but it means bit-exact reproducibility is not currently achievable.

One thing is not in doubt

Whatever the true values are, we currently publish every measure with no indication of how much data is behind it — a segment with 4,176 observations and one with 12 look identical in the table, on the map, and in any statewide roll-up. NPMRDS already tells us the answer in a column we load and ignore. Making confidence visible changes no reported value and is the cheapest improvement available.
