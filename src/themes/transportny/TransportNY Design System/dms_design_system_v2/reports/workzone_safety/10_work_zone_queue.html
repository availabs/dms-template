Work Zone Queues.

A speed measure says the work zone was slow. A queue measure says how far back the slow traffic reached, for how long, and how often. This phase walked every work zone's corridor upstream, five minutes at a time, and published the share of work zones whose queue ever reached more than 0.75 miles beyond the work, with the length, the duration and the share of time alongside. The finding that matters most is the same one the speed phase reached from the other side: a fixed speed line measures the road as much as the work, and the comparator that scales with the road is what makes the facility contrast honest.

What the measure says
Between a fifth and a third of zones, depending on the line

Against a fixed 35 mph line, 28.8% of measured work zones had a queue reach past 0.75 miles at some point. Against a line that scales with each road's posted limit it is 21.8%. The difference is almost entirely arterials, where a 35 mph line calls ordinary traffic a queue.

On Interstates the two lines agree; off them they do not

CY2024 Interstate zones spent 9.5% of active time queued on the fixed line and 8.4% on the road-scaled one. Off the Interstate system the fixed line says 40.4% and the road-scaled line 19.7% — and 66.5% of those arterial “queues” ran to the end of the corridor the walk could see.

69 of 220 significant candidates queued past the line

The rule's own sample in CY2024: 31.4% exceeded 0.75 mi (28.6% on the comparator), queues were present 12.6% of their active time, and the median longest queue among those that had one was 1.34 mi. Both lines agree here because they are Interstates by construction.

M3, as built

the connected-queue method · HOP-13-043 / Maryland WZPMA

For every five-minute period a work zone was active, the walk starts at the segment being worked on and asks three questions.

1
Is the bottleneck slow?

The anchor segment must be observed and below the line. A queue is measured from the bottleneck backwards; slow traffic upstream of a free-flowing work zone belongs to some other bottleneck.

2
How far back does it reach?

Walk upstream one segment at a time while each is observed and below the line. Queue length is the sum of those upstream segments — the work zone's own length is not counted. An unobserved segment ends the walk and marks the length a lower bound.

3
Is it real?

A queue counts only inside a run of two or more consecutive slow periods — ten minutes. One slow five-minute sample is noise, and Maryland's connected-queue rule filters it the same way.

Three forms of the result are published, because they answer different questions. The share of zones whose maximum queue passed the threshold is the rule's own shape — percent of projects over a line — and is the reported M3. The share of active time with a queue present is the hour-weighted programme figure. Length and duration — the maximum, the 95th percentile and the longest continuous run — are what a project review reads.

The line, in one row
queued = anchor < 35 mph
length = Σ upstream miles < 35 mph
M3 = max length > 0.75 mi

35 mph is Ohio's in-zone line; 0.75 mi is Ohio's allowable predicted queue. Illinois uses 1.5 mi beyond pre-existing. Every row carries the values it was measured with, so either is a re-run rather than a redesign.

The comparator on every row

The same walk is run against max(20, 0.6 × posted) per segment — the federal PHED anchor AVAIL's congestion series uses. It is 39 mph on a 65 mph Interstate and 20 mph on a 30 mph street, so it asks the same question everywhere. Phase 3 found the fixed line distorted the facility comparison for speeds; this is the check that it does for queues too.

What this means for the declared measure

The rule's programmatic review is of significant projects, which are Interstates by construction, and on Interstates the two lines agree. The 35 mph line is defensible there and it is what peers use. For a statewide figure that includes arterials, report the road-scaled comparator beside it or restrict the universe to freeways — a statewide 28.8% is mostly a statement about posted limits.

Interstate queues are longer, not more frequent

When an Interstate zone did queue, its longest queue had a median of 1.66 mi and its longest continuous run a median of 75 minutes, against 0.92 mi and 50 minutes off the system. Freeway queues are rarer, longer and last longer — which is what the physics says they should be.

TRANSCOM's event-to-segment conflation (view 2799) is the “incident view” New York already has: it walks an OpenStreetMap graph away from the event in every direction, following ramps and cross streets, and keeps every segment that carried delay. Only 20.9% of its impact segments are on the work zone's own road, and only 11.0% are upstream of it. On that comparable subset — same road, upstream — our walk found 69.2% of the segments TRANSCOM attributed. The two methods agree where they measure the same thing; the rest is TRANSCOM counting spill onto ramps and side streets, which this walk deliberately does not.

Why not just use TRANSCOM's extent

It is a delay attribution over the whole event, not a queue at a point in time: it has no length per five minutes, no duration, no 95th percentile, and it includes segments downstream of the work. It is the right input for M2 (delay), and phase 4 used it. M3 needs the shape of the queue, which only the speeds can give.

How the measure was computed

work_zone pipeline · stage “queue”

No new data was acquired for this phase. It joins the work-zone inventory from phase 1 to the 5-minute probe speeds New York already licenses through the National Performance Management Research Data Set, and to that data set's own roadway inventory, which orders every segment along its road.

1
Use the same active windows as the speed measure

When each zone was working, to the five minutes, from TRANSCOM's event-to-segment record where it exists and from the event's own clock times where it does not — the identical expansion the speed phase uses, now shared code, so M1 and M3 are measured over the same minutes.

2
Build each zone's upstream corridor

The inventory numbers segments along each road in the direction of travel — measured, not assumed: a segment's end meets the next-numbered segment's start in 69 % of pairs and the reverse in 13 %. So upstream is the lower number, on the same road and direction. Where two segments share a number (ramps beside the mainline) the longer is kept; the corridor stops at a gap over half a mile, ten miles back, or twenty segments.

3
Walk it, every five minutes, where the speeds are

A year is some five million active five-minute periods, each with up to twenty segments; the walk runs inside the speed database, one calendar month at a time. If the anchor is below the line, the contiguous run of upstream segments also below it is the queue; an unobserved segment ends the run and marks the length a lower bound. Both the fixed line and the road-scaled comparator are walked on every period.

4
Require two consecutive periods

A queue is present only inside a run of two or more consecutive slow periods at the anchor. One five-minute sample below the line is not a queue; ten minutes is. The share of time, the queue hours and the longest continuous run are all counted after this rule.

5
Publish the zone, keep the hours

One row per work zone carries the measure — maximum and 95th-percentile queue, share of time, longest run, the over-threshold flag under both lines, the corridor that was walked and how much of the length is a lower bound — together with the list of segments in the longest queue and a geometry of that extent, so the layer draws how far back the queue reached. A second table keeps one row per zone, date and clock hour as the evidence. The measure is rolled up per universe (all, week-plus, significant, Interstate, not Interstate) onto each vintage's metadata.

What NYSDOT still needs to decide
  1. The speed line. 35 mph is Ohio's and it is right for the Interstate projects the review covers. For anything statewide it over-counts arterials by a factor of 2.1×; the road-scaled comparator is on every row.
  2. The length line, and its basis. 0.75 mi of upstream queue beyond the work, not including the work zone's own length. Illinois measures “beyond pre-existing”, which needs the baseline the speed report also asked for.
  3. Maximum, or 95th percentile. The maximum is one five-minute period and, on rural roads, often one four-mile segment. The 95th percentile is on every row and is the more defensible number for a project review.
  4. Whether ramps count. This walk stays on the mainline; TRANSCOM's extent follows ramps and cross streets. A queue that spills onto a ramp is not measured here, and neither method sees a queue on a road the probe network does not cover.

Compiled 2026-09-16 for phase 5 of planning/transportny/tasks/current/workzone-performance-data-type-pipeline.md, generated from a live query of DAMA sources 2226 and 2227 on npmrds2 rather than hand-assembled, so it can be regenerated after any rebuild. M3 is the share of work zones whose maximum upstream queue exceeded 0.75 miles, where a queue is the contiguous run of segments upstream of the anchor with speeds below 35 mph while the anchor is below 35 mph in two or more consecutive five-minute periods; queue length excludes the anchor's own length. The PHED comparator repeats the walk against max(20, 0.6 × posted limit) per segment. Corridors are the segments on the anchor's own NPMRDS linear and direction with a lower road_order, the longest segment kept at a tied order, limited to 10 upstream miles, 20 segments and gaps under 0.5 mi. Figures are on the conflated active-window basis (window_source = '2799') unless stated; CY2019 and CY2020 exist only on the fallback basis and CY2026 covers 1 January to 31 August 2026 only. Speeds are derived from NPMRDS travel times as miles × 3600 / travel_time for all vehicle classes at the segment's inventory length for that year; posted limits come from the npmrds_geometry meta view in Postgres. The TRANSCOM comparison uses view 2799's impact segments for the same zones, restricted to the anchor's linear and lower orders.
