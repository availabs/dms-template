Work Zone Speed Impact.

The federal rule names one example mobility measure by title, and New York's declared form of it is the percent of active work-zone hours in which the average speed within the zone falls more than 10 mph below the posted limit, never below 20 mph. This phase computed that measure, and computed it three other ways alongside so the threshold stays reviewable. The finding that matters most is not the number: it is that the measure only means anything when it is reported against the same road's own history, on a named set of projects.

M1, as the rule defines it

CY2024 · significant candidates
the measure

Percent of active work-zone hours in which the average speed within the zone falls below the threshold.

Both highlighted phrases are load-bearing. The unit of time is an hour, classified once as below threshold or not — not a five-minute period. The unit of space is the zone, so the speed is the whole extent's average: total distance travelled divided by total travel time. That average is a harmonic one, which means it is pulled toward the congested part of a zone — correctly, because that is where drivers spend their time.

Executive reading

Across New York's 220 significant work-zone candidates in 2024, traffic ran more than 10 mph below the posted speed limit for 2,376 of 11,619 active hours — 20.5% of the time. On the same road segments, in the same hours of the day, a year earlier, the figure was 7.0%.

The work therefore multiplied the time these corridors spent well below their posted speed by 2.9×. That difference — not the 20.5% on its own — is the programme's mobility impact.

It replicates on an independent year. The same computation on CY2023's 271 significant candidates gives 24.1% during against 9.1% before — 2.6×, against 2.9× for CY2024 — and again slightly more than half the individual projects came in better than their own baseline. Two years, two independent zone sets, the same answer.

The aggregate and the typical project disagree

The programme total rose 2.9× — but 112 of 216 individual projects came in BETTER than their own baseline, against 99 that came in worse. 101 never dropped below the threshold at all, and the median project sits at 2.2%.

A small number of large impacts carries the total. Both figures are true and they support opposite headlines, which is exactly why neither should be published alone.

As a LEVEL, the measure inverts the answer

On the week-plus universe the 35 mph threshold reports 34.6% during — more than double the significant candidates' 15.2% — and would rank those zones the worse performers. But the same segments read 34.5% before any work began. The actual impact is +0.1 points: essentially nil. The level was reporting chronic congestion on slow roads, not work zones.

As a DIFFERENCE, the threshold barely matters

On the significant candidates all three thresholds agree on direction and rough magnitude — ratios of 2.9×, 3.2× and 3.5×. The threshold debate is far less consequential than it looks, provided the measure is reported against a baseline. It only dominates the answer when a bare level is published.

Posted−10 detects real change on slow roads

On the mixed-facility week-plus universe, posted−10 still finds +3.8 points of added slow time where the fixed 35 mph threshold finds +0.1. Because it is calibrated to each road's own limit it can still see a change on roads that are slow to begin with — which is the strongest argument for it over the alternatives.

A statewide percentage over our universe is therefore not comparable to any figure a peer publishes, and it is dominated by short maintenance activity.

What Ohio actually publishes

Not a statewide share. ODOT's central office runs speed analysis on 25-30 key projects a season and charts the number of hours below 35 mph, per project, per month, with reference lines for the same months in the two years before construction, and annotations for incidents, weather and events. Monthly cadence, plus a speed heatmap by month and time of day.

FHWA's own NPMRDS guidance (HOP-20-029) reports the same shape: a count of underperforming hours, a per-month rate so unequal periods compare, a baseline row computed identically, a per-month breakdown, and the average severity during the bad hours.

Five rules for reporting M1
  1. Never publish a level alone. A bare percentage reports the road as much as the work, and on one of our universes it reverses the ranking entirely.
  2. Always name the universe and its zone count. "31.8% of work-zone hours" over 38,000 mostly-six-hour shifts is not the same statement as "20.5% over 216 significant projects".
  3. Pair the share with a count of hours. 2,376 hours below threshold is actionable; a percentage alone hides whether that is three hours or three thousand.
  4. Report the aggregate and the typical project together, because they disagree here and each supports a different headline.
  5. Report per project, monthly, for the significant tier — Ohio's and FHWA's form. The statewide series over 216 zones is too small to be stable: it moved between 7% and 35% across our nine vintages purely on composition.
One caution on the posted-limit threshold, on the record

NCHRP Synthesis 482 (Work Zone Speed Management) warns that comparing observed speeds to the posted limit alone is confounded, and the state-practice review found that among peers nobody uses the posted limit as the sole reference — all of them compare against pre-construction conditions on the same segments. The confound is that work zones frequently carry a reduced posted limit, so "below the posted limit" can measure compliance with a temporary restriction rather than mobility impact.

Our posted limits come from the roadway inventory, which carries each segment's normal limit, not the temporary work-zone limit. So posted−10 here measures "traffic ran more than 10 mph below what this road is normally posted for" — a defensible impact measure, and the one of the three that best survives being applied across facility types. It is not a compliance measure, and it should not be described as one. Reporting it against the pre-construction baseline, as above, neutralises the confound: whatever the posted limit does or does not represent, it is the same number on both sides of the comparison.

What the measure says
Just under a third of work-zone time is more than 10 mph down

Across the five comparable years, 30.4% of observed work-zone time ran more than 10 mph below the posted limit — mean observed speed 45.5 mph against a mean threshold of 40.5. Measured on the road's own limit, the number is a statement about work zones rather than about which roads happened to be worked on.

It halves the distortion a fixed threshold creates

A fixed 35 mph line makes off-Interstate work zones look 4.5× worse than Interstate ones. Against the posted limit that falls to 2.2×, on roads whose observed speeds differ by 0.7×. Most of what the fixed threshold was measuring was the speed limit.

Night work is 2.3× cheaper

Work at 04:00 runs below threshold 16.0% of the time against 36.4% at 08:00 — yet active hours peak at 15:00. Phase 2 could not see this at all; it spread every zone's hours evenly across the day.

Read the bottom row. The two kinds of road differ by 0.7× in what they are posted for and 0.7× in the speed actually observed. A fixed 35 mph line turns that into a 4.5× difference in measured performance; the posted−10 threshold turns it into 2.2×. The closer that ratio is to the 0.7× the roads actually differ by, the more of what is left is work zones rather than speed limits.

That is the whole argument for reporting against the posted limit. A fixed threshold sits far below normal on an Interstate and right on top of normal on an arterial, so it cannot be compared across facility types, Regions, or years in which the mix of work changes. Asking “did traffic drop more than 10 mph below what this road is posted for” asks the same question everywhere.

The threshold in one line
max(20, posted − 10)

Capped at the posted limit itself, so a segment posted below 20 mph cannot be measured against a threshold above its own limit.

The 20 mph floor is the same one FHWA's congestion measure uses, and it is not a rounding detail: it binds on 18,247 of 251,143 cells — every segment posted at or below 30 mph.

Why the statewide totals look alike

Statewide, posted−10 (30.1%) and the fixed 35 mph (32.3%) land within a point of each other, because the average posted limit is around 48 mph and so the average threshold is around 38. The two measures differ in where they find slow time, not in how much they find — which is why the facility split above, not the total, is the reason to prefer one.

The significant-candidate population is 2.7% of measured work-zone hours. Phase 1 found the rule is driven by lane-closure reporting, which varies from 92% of work zones in one Region to 25% in another — so this sample is shaped as much by paperwork as by the road.

The bottom row is the clearest single argument in this report. These are 2,741 work zones on streets posted at or below 30 mph, whose normal operating speed is 23.6 mph. A fixed 35 mph threshold flags 86.1% of their active time as an exceedance — it is measuring the street, not the work zone. Held at the 20 mph floor, the same time reads 39.6%.

Where the floor does not bind, the two thresholds are close (29.3% against 28.0%) because the mean threshold there, 41.7 mph, is not far from 35. Nearly all of the difference between the two measures statewide comes from this one band of slow roads.

Why not let it go lower

Without a floor, a street posted at 25 mph would be measured against 15 mph — a speed at which traffic is stopped rather than delayed — and the measure would fall silent exactly where local work is most disruptive to the people living on it.

The floor is also capped at the posted limit itself, so a segment posted below 20 mph is never measured against a threshold above its own limit. That affects four segments statewide, and without the cap each would have reported every observation as an exceedance.

What it is worth

Mean observed speed runs 50.3 mph in the quietest hour against 42.4 mph in the worst, on segments whose free-flow reference barely moves (53.9 to 52.4). The difference is congestion, not geometry — the part a scheduling decision can change.

The active window — when a work zone was actually working, to the five minutes — comes from TRANSCOM's event-to-segment conflation, and that conflation has no rows at all for 2019 or 2020 and only 9,070 events in 2018 against 134,337 in 2024. Nothing in the work-zone inventory is missing for those years; what is missing is the sub-daily window.

Those years are still published, using each zone's own reported start and end times as the window, and every row records which source it used. The fallback is sound in the middle and badly wrong in the tail: on CY2024, where both bases exist side by side, the median zone gets 5.3 active hours against the conflated 4.8 — but the 90th percentile is 218.6 hours against 16.0, because the fallback window is the whole chain's span rather than a per-segment one, so a recurring chain claims weeks it was not working. The two bases are not interchangeable and this report does not blend them.

On the five comparable years this evidence share moves from 32.5% in 2021 to 30.0% in 2025. CY2026 is partial and its conflation is still filling in.

CY2020 is a sanity check that passed

On the fallback basis, CY2020 is the lowest year on every threshold — 22.3% on the posted−10 threshold against 27.3% in 2019 and 28.9% in 2021. Nothing in the pipeline knows about the pandemic; a speed-exceedance measure simply ought to bottom out in the year traffic collapsed, and it does.

CY2021's baseline could not be cleaned

The context baseline removes days on which a segment carried any other work zone. CY2021's baseline year is 2020 — where the conflation has no rows — so nothing could be excluded. That leaves its baseline slightly slow. It does not touch the threshold measures, which reads the posted limit and never the baseline.

Missing periods are dropped, not filled

A measurable cell averages 19.7 observations — never the 288 a full day would hold, because the probe data has gaps. The denominator is what was observed. How the measure was computed

work_zone pipeline · stage “speed”

No new data was acquired for this phase. It joins the work-zone inventory from phase 1 to the 5-minute probe speeds New York already licenses through the National Performance Management Research Data Set, and to the speed percentiles AVAIL already computes from them for the federal performance measures.

1
Work out when each zone was actually working

Not when it was open on the calendar — when it was active, to the five minutes. Each work zone's reported window is expanded onto the day grid, so a zone running four nights is measured on four nights and not on the daytimes between them, and a shift that crosses midnight is measured on both sides of it.

2
Measure only the segments being worked on

A work zone's record also names the segments downstream where congestion was attributed to it. Those are excluded, exactly as in the exposure phase: including them would count the queue a work zone causes as part of the work zone, and would make every zone look slower than it is.

3
Turn travel times into speeds

The probe data records how long vehicles took to traverse a segment, not how fast they were going, so every speed is derived from the segment's length — and therefore from the right year's roadway inventory. Five-minute periods with no observation are left out of the denominator rather than filled in: the measure is a share of time, and inventing the missing periods would invent the answer.

4
Compare against the road's own posted limit

The reported measure asks whether traffic dropped more than 10 mph below what the road is posted for, with the threshold never allowed below 20 mph — the same floor the federal congestion measure uses, because below that traffic is stopped rather than delayed. Three other thresholds are computed on every row as comparators, so the choice can be revisited from the evidence without rebuilding the dataset.

5
Keep the hour of day

Results are stored per work zone, per segment, per hour of day — pooling every active day into 24 buckets rather than keeping every date. That is what lets the same dataset answer “how bad was this zone” and “is night work actually cheaper” without storing a row per five minutes, and it is the grain the speed-differential measure will need.

What NYSDOT still needs to decide
  1. The drop, and the floor. Ten mph below posted, floored at 20, is the chosen threshold and it is what this report leads with. Both numbers are parameters — every run stamps the values that produced its figures, so a different drop is a re-run, not a redesign.
  2. A share, or a count of zones over a line. The distribution is bimodal, so no average describes a typical zone. “9,931 work zones spent more than half their active time below threshold” is both truer and more actionable than any mean.
  3. Whether the significance rule stays as it is. Significant candidates score 20.5% against a statewide 30.1%, and a Queens zone that ran well below its threshold is not flagged at all. Measuring the rule's own sample will not describe the programme.
  4. Whether to ask TRANSCOM for the 2019–2020 conflation. It is the only thing standing between a five-year series and a nine-year one, and the underlying events are already held.

M1 is the share of active work-zone HOURS in which the ZONE's average speed — total distance over total travel time across its whole extent — fell below max(20, posted speed limit − 10 mph), capped at the posted limit. The 20 mph floor binds on every segment posted at or below 30 mph and matches the floor FHWA's own PHED threshold uses. The sections from “the evidence underneath” onward are NOT M1: they report the share of individual five-minute periods on individual segments, which is finer in both time and space and answers where and when inside a zone the slow time fell. Figures are on the conflated active-window basis (window_source = '2799') unless stated; CY2019 and CY2020 exist only on the fallback basis and CY2026 covers 1 January to 31 August 2026 only. Speeds are derived from NPMRDS travel times as miles × 3600 / travel_time for all vehicle classes, at the segment's metadata length for that year. Posted limits come from the npmrds_geometry meta view in Postgres, not from the ClickHouse copy of the field, which is empty — the existing 2021–2025 excessive-delay series was computed against that empty field and effectively used a uniform 20 mph threshold, so it is not directly comparable. Free-flow comparators are the PM3 85th-percentile speeds (DAMA source 2135), AVAIL's own PM3 computation rather than the federally submitted MAP-21 product.
