The New York Work Zone Universe.

Before New York can measure work-zone safety and mobility under 23 CFR 630 Subpart J, it needs to know what its work zones are: how many, where, for how long, and how reliably they are recorded. Phase 1 of the work_zone pipeline built that inventory — one row per real work zone, assembled from nine years of TRANSCOM event reports. This report is the executive view of it: the totals, the eight-year trend, the construction season, and — just as importantly — the three things the dataset cannot yet tell you.

what one row means
A work zone, not an event — and not a project

TRANSCOM reports a recurring work zone once per occurrence: a nightly lane closure for three months arrives as sixty separate event records. Counting those as sixty work zones would overstate the program roughly two-fold. Each row here is one chain of occurrences — the same activity, facility, direction, county and description, with no break longer than fourteen days — carrying the union of its active windows and the list of TRANSCOM event ids it absorbed. Statewide that collapses 772,284 event records into 355,732 work zones.

A chain is still not a project. One capital project whose nightly activity changes — milling, then paving, then shoulder work — is several chains. Tying work zones to project identity is a separate problem, measured in phase 1a and unresolved for the reason given below.

The volume is flat

New York records between 36,000 and 43,000 work zones a year, and has done for nine years. Nothing in the series suggests a growing or shrinking programme; the 2018–2019 dip is the tail of TRANSCOM’s own coverage ramp, not less work.

What did change: reporting

Lane-closure reporting went from 8% of work zones in 2018 to 63% in 2025. That single column governs how many zones can qualify as significant, so the apparent jump in significant candidates after 2020 is a reporting change, not a change on the road.

Significant work zones

the rule’s own floor, applied to CY2024

§630.1010 asks each agency to identify significant projects, and the rule’s floor is an Interstate project inside a TMA occupying a location more than three days with lane closures. Applied to CY2024:

… on an Interstate16,95039.7%

… and ≥3 consecutive days of any activity5201.2%
… and ≥3 consecutive days with a reported lane closure2200.5%

220 for a whole state in a year sounds small, so it was checked a second way. Measuring the rule’s own unit instead — consecutive days of work-zone activity at a location, rather than per chain — gives 180 Interstate locations with three or more consecutive days, 58 with a week or more, and 18 occupied for a month or more (longest: 187 days). Two independent units landing at 180–220 is what makes the figure credible.

finding
The significant list is regionally biased — by a reporting gap, not by the road

Region 8 (Hudson Valley) returns zero significant work zones across nine years and 51,218 work zones. It is not that the Hudson Valley has no sustained Interstate work: it is that only 25.4% of its work zones carry a lane-closure count, against 72.5% in New York City.

NYSDOT cannot defend a significant-project list built on TRANSCOM lane counts until that reporting is consistent. The dataset therefore publishes both the rule as written and an any_activity variant — 520 rather than 220 in CY2024 — to bound what the gap hides. Closing it is a programme action, not a modelling one.

A second fragility: how the work is described

The Thruway Authority carries 41,000 Interstate work zones on I-90 and I-87 and reports lane closures on 88% of them — and produces zero significant candidates, because each night’s work is described differently, so no chain ever spans three consecutive days. The rule’s three-day test is measured on whatever unit the reporting produces. That is why this report gives the location-based cross-check as well, and why phase 9 will need a project-level unit rather than a chain-level one.

Three things this dataset cannot tell you yet

1
How many hours of work

Active hours are summed from TRANSCOM’s own estimated_duration_mins, and that field is dominated by records that were never closed — single events of up to 1,000,000 minutes (about two years). Events longer than 30 days supply 40–53% of all recorded hours in 2018–2024 and 77–81% in 2025–2026, while the median duration barely moves (420→480 minutes). So the raw 18.6 million-hour total in this dataset is not a usable trend; capped at 30 days the series reads 0.9–1.2 M hours a year through 2024. Phase 2 must settle a duration basis before exposure can be published.

2
How long the queue was

Each work zone is anchored to the one TMC TRANSCOM matched it to, and separately carries the congestion-derived TMCs downstream of it. Those impact extents exist for 2021 onward but are absent for 2019 and 2020 entirely and thin for 2018, because the upstream event×TMC expansion does not cover those years. Anything built on the impact extent — delay, queues — starts in 2021.

3
Which project it belongs to

No work zone in this dataset carries a project PIN. Phase 1a acquired the NYSDOT capital programme and measured the join: only 27.8% of work-zone events can be tied to a project at defensible confidence, NYSDOT does not put PINs in TRANSCOM (28 of 70,490 events), and a spatial match carries no schedule information — 2024 events match the 2026–29 STIP better than 2026 events do. Project identity therefore enriches work zones; it cannot define them. The crosswalk lands in phase 9, on top of that verdict.

How this dataset was created

work_zone pipeline · stage “spine”

The inventory is derived, not collected. Its only substantive input is the TRANSCOM event store AVAIL already holds — source 956 / view 1947, 3.7 M events across NY, NJ and CT since 2015 — plus the NPMRDS roadway metadata that places events on the network. Five steps turn one into the other, each with its own unit tests and each re-runnable over any window.

1
Decide what is a work zone

Not by the obvious filter. The category field the TSMO dashboards use is not unique across the taxonomy’s levels — “Emergency Operations” sits under Incident, so filtering on it sweeps in police activity and vehicle fires. Classification instead keys on the source’s event_type over an exhaustive 44-value map, into construction, maintenance, utility, winter operations, other operations and incident response. The first three are in scope; scope itself is a parameter, because whether street sweeping and plowing count is NYSDOT’s decision. An event type the map does not recognise is reported, never silently included or dropped.

2
Collapse recurring chains

TRANSCOM carries no field linking a work zone’s occurrences — of 93,112 NY work-zone events in 2024, the candidate link fields are populated on 3, 0, 448 and 1 respectively. Chains are therefore inferred from activity, facility, direction, county and description, and split wherever the chain goes quiet for more than fourteen days, since a fortnight’s silence means a new mobilisation. Statewide this is a 2.17× collapse.

3
Place it on the network

Each event carries exactly one matched TMC — an anchor, not an extent. The event×TMC expansion supplies more, but it is built from congestion data and so runs downstream with the queue. The two are kept apart by role: the anchor is where the work is, the impact set is where the effect is felt. 97.1% of work zones are placed.

4
Test significance

Interstate status comes from the facility name, not the network functional class — that field is inherited from whichever TMC matched, and its “Interstates” include 43rd Street and 6th Avenue. TMA membership comes from the NPMRDS urbanized-area code, which is boundary-accurate rather than whole-county, and whose New York areas above the 200,000 threshold are exactly the six TMAs NYSDOT’s own Highway Design Manual names.

5
Publish one vintage per year

Each calendar year is written as its own dataset vintage, so a year can be rebuilt without touching the others, and re-running a window replaces it rather than duplicating it. Every work zone keeps the TRANSCOM event ids it absorbed, the thresholds and parameters the run used, and the provenance of its location — so any number in this report can be traced back to the event records behind it.

Reconciliation

The published TSMO work-zone figure for 2024 is 93,112 events. This pipeline reproduces that number exactly under the legacy filter, and reports 89,583 as its own in-scope population — the difference being 3,540 incident-response, winter-operations and drawbridge events removed, and 11 gas-main-break events added. Both counts are carried on every vintage so the two can always be reconciled.
