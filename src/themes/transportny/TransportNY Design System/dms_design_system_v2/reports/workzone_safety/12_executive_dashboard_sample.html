Work Zone Performance · Sampled Programme.

The same measures on a documented sample of 207 projects rather than on 41,616 event-derived shifts. Peer states report on a named set — Ohio monitors 25–30 key projects a season, Montana reviews up to 25 — and §630.1010's programmatic review asks New York for exactly that. The frame here is the rule's own: significant-project candidates, applied identically to every year so the series is comparable, with the wider monitored-programme tier reported alongside it.

The sample frame

declared once, applied identically to every year
the criteria

A zone is in the sample if it is a significant-project candidate: on the Interstate system, inside a Transportation Management Area, with a lane closure on three or more consecutive days.

This is not an analyst's choice of interesting projects. It is §630.1010(c)'s own definition of a significant project, as New York implements it in Highway Design Manual §16.5.2.1, evaluated from the work-zone inventory by a single published flag (is_significant_candidate) that is stamped on every zone in every vintage. Applying a rule rather than a judgement is what makes the year-on-year series comparable and the 2030 programmatic review defensible.

All 207 of 2025's candidates are Interstate and all are inside a TMA, by construction of the flag. The frame is therefore a census of a defined stratum, not a random sample, and it says nothing about the non-Interstate network — which the statewide dashboard covers and where exceedance is in fact higher.

Why a frame at all

New York's work-zone inventory is event-derived, so its unit is a shift, not a project: the median zone spans 5.7 hours. Reporting a programme measure over 41,616 such records answers a question no peer asks and no engineer can act on.

The sample is small and its size moves year to year — 218 in 2021, 307 in 2022, 207 in 2025 — because it is a consequence of what was built, not a quota. Year-on-year movement in any measure below is therefore partly a change in the projects and only partly a change in performance; with a frame this size, single-year swings should be read as a prompt to look at the named projects, not as a programme result. The week-plus tier (a zone whose span reaches 168 hours) is reported alongside as the wider monitored programme, and is the closest analogue in size to Illinois's project count.

M3 — queue exceedance, on the sample

2025 headline, then the 5-year trend
the programme measure

Percent of projects whose maximum upstream queue exceeded 0.75 miles.

A queue is the run of contiguous segments upstream of the zone travelling below 35 mph, and it must persist two consecutive five-minute periods before it counts — the Maryland “connected queue” rule, so one slow reading is not a queue. The threshold is Ohio's. Queues are the impact the public sees and the one a zone-only speed measure cannot show, because the queue forms outside the zone.

What this sample can and cannot support

limitations, stated up front
It is a stratum, not a sample

Every zone meeting the criteria is included, so there is no sampling error — but also no inference to the rest of the network. All 207 are Interstate and inside a TMA. Non-Interstate exceedance is materially higher (37.4% against 21.8%) and this frame does not see it.

The unit is a zone, not yet a project

Zones are chains of recurring TRANSCOM events, not STIP projects, so one contract may appear as several rows and a row may cover several contracts. The crosswalk to the State's project identifiers is phase 9 of the pipeline; until it lands, project counts here are zone counts and should be described as such.

The levels have no baseline

Peers compare each project against the same segments before construction. That comparison is not in the published dataset; phase 3 computed it ad hoc and found the sample's exceedance ran roughly three times its own pre-construction rate. Until it is published per vintage, every M1 level here is a level and not an impact.

How these measures were computed

work_zone pipeline · stages speed / delay / queue

No data was acquired for this dashboard. It reads the measures the work_zone pipeline publishes to DAMA from data New York already holds: TRANSCOM construction and maintenance events (the store behind 511NY) for when and where each zone was active, and the NPMRDS probe speeds and travel times New York receives free from FHWA for what traffic did there. It reports the sampled programme: 2025's 207 significant-project candidates, selected by a published flag rather than by judgement and applied identically to every vintage.

1
Work out when each zone was actually working

Not when it was open on the calendar — when it was active, to the five minutes. Each zone's reported spans are expanded onto the day grid, so a zone running four nights is measured on four nights and not on the daytimes between them, and a shift crossing midnight is measured on both sides of it.

2
Each measure is taken where its effect falls

M1 uses only the segments being worked on — counting the queue a zone causes as part of the zone would make every zone look slower than it is. M2 is the inverse and includes the downstream segments, because that is where the delay falls: 91.4% of the headline year's work-zone delay is on them. M3 looks strictly upstream, walking out from the zone's first segment along the corridor, because a queue is by definition what forms behind a restriction.

3
Thresholds are declared, not discovered

M1 asks whether the zone's average speed fell more than 10 mph below what the road is normally posted for, never below 20 mph — the floor FHWA's own congestion measure uses, because below it traffic is stopped rather than delayed. M2 flags zones above 10 minutes of delay per vehicle. M3 flags a maximum upstream queue over 0.75 miles, built from contiguous segments under 35 mph that stay slow for two consecutive five-minute periods. Every threshold is stamped on the published view.

4
A missing input is published as unknown, never as zero

5,435 of the headline year's 41,616 zones have no delay conflation and are excluded from the denominator rather than averaged in as zeros. The same rule governs M1 and M3: five-minute periods with no probe observation are left out of the denominator, not filled in.

5
The measures want a baseline that the dataset does not yet carry

Phase 3 established the central finding of this programme: M1 means something as a difference against the same segments' own pre-construction history, and very little as a level. On our week-plus universe a fixed 35 mph threshold reads almost identically before and during the work — the level was reporting chronic congestion on slow roads, not work zones. That baseline is not stored in the published dataset. It is computed on demand by an analysis script and is scheduled for the work_zone_measures rollup, so every level on this dashboard is published without one and must be read as an exposure-weighted level, not an impact — M3's queue counts included, since New York's urban corridors queue without any work zone at all. See 09 — work-zone speed impact for the during-vs-before analysis on CY2023 and CY2024.

6
Why the series starts at 2021

The event→segment conflation that puts a zone on the road network has no rows at all for 2019 and 2020 and only 2.3% coverage in 2018, so those vintages are not comparable and are not shown. 2026 covers 1 January to 31 August only and is reported separately, never averaged into a trend. On the headline year the conflation reaches 35,746 of 39,714 measurable zones (90.0%); the remainder fall back to the event's own reported window, which is the chain's span rather than the shift's, and the fallback is excluded from every supporting cut on this page.
