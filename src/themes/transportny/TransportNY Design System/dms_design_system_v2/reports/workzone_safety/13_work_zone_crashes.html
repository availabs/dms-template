Work Zone Crashes.

The rule's safety measure, from the State's crash system of record. A crash can be tied to a work zone two ways — the police report codes one, or the crash sits inside one while it is active — and this phase computed both so they could check each other. They barely overlap: of 1,335 coded crashes in CY2024, 186 fall inside a work zone the inventory knows was active; of 9,112 crashes that do, 186 carry the code. Which of those is the measure is the question this report is about.

What the measure says
The code and the location name different crashes

Only 13.9% of the 1,335 police-coded work-area crashes fall inside a work zone the TRANSCOM inventory knows was active at the time (30.9% fall inside one at any time in its span). And only 2.0% of the 9,112 crashes located inside an active zone were coded. Neither number is the truth about the other; together they bound it.

Crash density in active hours is about the same as in off-hours

Within the same zones' own spans, crashes ran at 13.4 per 1,000 active hours against 14.2 per 1,000 hours when the zone was not working. On Interstates 12.3 against 17.9; off them 13.9 against 12.8. The work zone's hours were not the road's most dangerous hours — but this control knows nothing about volume, and Interstate work happens at night when traffic is light. The like-for-like rate needs the baseline that comes next.

Most located crashes are on the approach, not the work

85.7% of located crashes sit on the upstream queue segments rather than the segment being worked on. That is where a queue's rear end is, and it is why the rate over the work extent alone (354 per 100 M VMT) and the rate with the approach counted in (1,079) are different numbers — the second has no approach VMT under it and is an upper bound.

“Crashes in zone” counts zone–crash pairs: 11,489 pairs are 9,112 distinct crashes, because 1,638 crashes sat inside two active zones at once (adjacent shifts on one corridor). The headline uses the distinct count; the per-zone rows and rates use each zone's own. Read the two rate columns as different measures. The work-extent rate puts crashes on the segment being worked on over the vehicle-miles that segment carried while active — numerator and denominator on the same road. The all-roles rate adds the crashes on the upstream approach but has no approach VMT to add underneath (phase 2 measured exposure on the work extent only), so it is an upper bound and rises with how far back a queue reached. The last two columns are the volume-free comparison: crashes per thousand hours the zone was working, against crashes per thousand hours inside the same zones' spans when they were not. Where the active-hours figure is the higher one, the work zone's hours were the more dangerous hours on that road.

A crash inside an active work zone is not necessarily caused by it: on a busy urban freeway the approach segments carry crashes whether or not anyone is working. That is why the off-hours comparison above exists. But 98.0% of these crashes carrying no code is also NYSDOT's own research finding — work-zone crashes are under-coded at the scene — and it is the reason a located measure has to exist beside the coded one.

Which is M5?

Both, declared as what they are. The coded count is the statewide series: comparable to every other state, to NYSDOT's own reports and to the open data, and biased low by under-coding. The located count and its rates are the project-level measure the 2030 review needs — per zone, per severity, with a denominator — and biased high by crashes that would have happened anyway. The programme should publish the coded series as the headline and the located rates per project, and say why they differ.

Every crash near a zone's extent inside its span is kept in wz_crash_match; only those inside an active window count. The rows outside the window are the control: the same road, the same weeks, the hours nobody was working. Per thousand hours, active time carried 13.4 crashes and off-time 14.2 — about the same. On the significant candidates it is 11.2 against 18.2 (1.6× lower); on Interstates 12.3 against 17.9 (1.5× lower); off the Interstate system 13.9 against 12.8 (about the same).

Two cautions. Off-hours are mostly night and weekend, when traffic is lighter, so part of any gap is exposure rather than the work; a like-for-like baseline (the same segments, the same hours of day, the year before — the comparison the speed report asked for) is the next step and is what the match table exists to feed. And the coded column shows the coding is not random: of the coded crashes near a zone, most are in or within an hour of its window.

What “in a work zone” means here

Within 50 m of the centreline of the segment being worked on, or of the upstream segments phase 5 measured as queued at the zone's longest queue, on a date inside the zone's span, at a time inside one of its reported active windows. The buffer is a parameter and is stamped on every row; the windows are the same minutes the speed and queue measures used.

What the zone’s span is

From its first reported start to its last reported end. A recurring maintenance chain spans weeks but works a few hours a night; a one-day closure's span is its window. Off-hours therefore mean different things on different zones, which is why the comparison is shown per universe and not as one number.

CLEAR is seven months behind

Crashes reach CLEAR through DMV, months after the event. The newest record in the CY2024 file was inserted on 2026-02-05; nothing later exists in either year's file. CY2024 is settled — December is its highest month. CY2025 is not: 253,218 crashes against 377,778 in CY2024, the second half of the year roughly half-missing. It is loaded and typed as a provisional vintage (1,168 coded so far) and should be re-pulled before it is reported. The 2021–2023 pulls for a trend are one downloader run each.

What the located measure cannot say

That the work zone caused the crash. It says the crash happened inside the zone's extent during its hours. On a segment that crashes every rush hour that is a low bar, and the approach segments of a long queue are exactly such segments. The off-hours comparison is a first control; the proper one is the same segments' crash history before the work, which is phase 10's rollup. The coded series has the opposite problem — an officer has to have written it down — and the two bracket the truth.

How the measure was computed

work_zone pipeline · stages “crashes_clear” and “crash_join”

One new dataset: the NYSDOT CLEAR Crash Data Viewer, pulled statewide for a whole year by the extractor in references/workzone_saftey/clear/ using the owner's own access — every crash-level field, with coordinates converted from UTM 18N. It is joined to the work-zone inventory from phase 1, the exposure from phase 2 and the queue extents from phase 5.

1
Type every crash once

KABCO from the maximum injury (property damage becomes O), a severity class, the five-minute period of the day, the work-zone code from the traffic-control field, the flagger code beside it, and the FHWA functional class mapped from CLEAR's own codes — CLEAR's code 7 is the urban Interstate. Unknown counts stay unknown.

2
Use the same active windows as the speed and queue measures

When each zone was working, to the five minutes — the identical expansion the earlier phases used, so M1, M3 and M5 are measured over the same minutes.

3
Search from each zone’s extent

The extent is the segment being worked on plus, where phase 5 measured a queue, the upstream segments it reached. Every crash within 50 m of it on a date inside the zone's span is a candidate; the distance to the work segment decides whether it sits on the work or on the approach; the active windows decide whether it counts.

4
Count, and divide only where there is something to divide by

Per zone: crashes by role and severity, the coded and flagger counts among them, the crashes found nearby outside the hours. The rate per 100 million vehicle-miles uses phase 2's vehicle-miles through the work extent, and is computed only where that exposure is complete. A time-based rate — crashes per thousand active hours — is published beside it because it needs no volume.

5
Keep every match

One row per crash and zone it was near, in the window or not, with the distance, the role and the gap to the nearest window — the evidence under the per-zone table, the material for the off-hours comparison here, and the input to the before-and-after baseline that comes next.

What NYSDOT still needs to decide
  1. Which M5 to declare. The coded series is comparable and low; the located rates are project-level and high. Declare the coded count as the statewide safety measure and report located crashes and rates per significant project, with the difference explained.
  2. The buffer. 50 m from the centreline catches 63.4% of located crashes within 10 m; the parameter is stamped on every row and a different buffer is a re-run.
  3. Whether the approach counts. 85.7% of located crashes are on the queue approach. MMUCC's definition includes the queue; the exposure denominator does not yet.
  4. The baseline. The same segments, the same hours of day, the year before — the comparison M1 already needs. Until then the off-hours comparison is the control.
  5. Raising CLEAR's lag with NYSDOT. Seven months stale makes the current year unreportable until the following autumn.
