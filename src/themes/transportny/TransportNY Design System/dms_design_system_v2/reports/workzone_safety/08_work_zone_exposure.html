Work Zone Exposure.

“220 significant work zones” means very little on its own. “220 work zones exposing 85 million vehicle-miles of traffic” is a programme. Every measure the Subpart J rule asks for is a rate — crashes per hundred million vehicle-miles, delay per vehicle — and this phase built the denominators: how many lanes were closed, for how many lane-mile-hours, and how much traffic drove through. It also had to decide what “for how long” even means, because the field that answers it cannot be taken at face value.

One of these is reportable

VMT through work zones is steady — between 546 and 764 million a year across eight complete years — because it needs only a traffic count and a length, and both are recorded consistently.

Lane-mile-hours rises seven-fold over the same period, from 194,000 to 1.4 million. Almost none of that is more work: it needs a reported lane count, and lane-closure reporting went from 2,915 work zones in 2018 to 26,221 in 2025. E2 is currently a reporting series wearing the clothes of an exposure series.

Where it can be trusted

Use exposure_complete before aggregating. Within the population the rule governs — Interstate work zones in urbanised areas — coverage is essentially total: 95.2% of significant candidates have both E2 and E3.

The hardest number: how long

and why it is a parameter, not a constant

Lane-mile-hours is literally hours × lanes × length, so the duration field decides the measure. And that field cannot be taken at face value: TRANSCOM's reported durations include records that were opened and never closed — single events of up to 1,051,059 minutes, about two years. Events longer than thirty days supply 40–53% of all reported hours in 2018–2024 and 77–81% in 2025–2026, while the median duration barely moves at 420–480 minutes.

So exposure computes on a declared basis, recorded on every row, and always publishes the duration-independent variant beside it:

default
reported, capped per occurrence

Each occurrence is clipped at thirty days, so a sixty-night chain of normal shifts is untouched while one runaway record is not allowed to carry a whole work zone. 3,348 work zones had at least one occurrence capped.

variant
nominal shift

Reported duration ignored entirely: one eight-hour shift per active day. Computed on every row, whatever basis the run used, so the sensitivity is never hidden.

comparison
reported, uncapped

The field as TRANSCOM gives it. Available for comparison; not recommended for publication.

What the choice is worth

Lane-mile-hours under each basis, all nine vintages:

The capped basis is 2.24× the nominal one, so the choice is not cosmetic — it more than doubles the headline. Even at thirty days the cap is generous: it still admits a single occurrence of 720 hours, and the capped series still carries some of the reporting artefact (2025 and 2026 sit 40% above 2021–2024 on the capped basis while moving in the opposite direction on the nominal one).

So the honest position is that lane-mile-hours is not yet a year-on-year measure, on any basis. It is reliable within a year and between places; it is not yet reliable across years. NYSDOT should pick a basis before the December 2026 policy deadline — we would take the nominal shift, as the only one insensitive to a field that cannot be trusted — and the capped variant should be published beside it until closure reporting improves.

What can and cannot be measured

Exposure needs three things per work zone: a lane count, a length, and a traffic volume. Length is essentially always there. The other two are not, and where they are missing the measure is published as unknown rather than zero — a zero would quietly drag every average down.

Anchors, not queues

Exposure counts only the segment the work sits on. The congestion-derived segments downstream of a zone — where the queue forms — are excluded by design; counting them would inflate exposure by the length of the traffic jam the work causes.

The significant subset is 0.4% of work zones and carries 1.5% of measured VMT through work zones — a concentration of about three and a half times, which is what one would expect from a rule that selects for sustained closures on the busiest Interstates. It is a real concentration but a modest one: governing the programme through a sample of significant projects reaches the most-exposed work, not most of the exposure.

Is the number the right size?

checked against HPMS, and against itself
Against the state's own travel

A derived exposure figure is only believable if it sits sensibly inside the total travel it is a part of. New York's HPMS submission for 2024 gives:

Work zones expose about 0.87% of NHS travel in a year. The phase plan guessed a single-digit percentage; the measured answer is at the low end of that, which is what the arithmetic demands — a work zone occupies a short stretch of road for a few hours at a time, and there are only about forty thousand of them a year.

Against itself

An outside-in check can still hide an internal error, so the CY2024 figures were reconstructed a second way. 33,960 work zones with a volume, averaging 1.87 active days at 6.9 hours a day, gives roughly 440,000 zone-hours. Dividing the 544 million vehicles across those gives about 1,235 vehicles per hour of active work zone.

That is the right order for the roads work zones sit on: a segment carrying 20,000 vehicles a day in one direction averages a little over 800 an hour, and rather more in the daytime hours when most work is scheduled. The two routes to the number agree, which is the point of doing both.

what would falsify this

Hour-of-day. Exposure currently spreads a zone's active hours evenly across the day, because the spine records how long a zone ran but not when. Night work — which most lane closures are — sees materially less traffic than the daily average implies, so E3 is more likely to be an over-estimate than an under-estimate. Phase 3 reads speeds by five-minute epoch, and will make an hour-of-day exposure possible for the first time.

How exposure was computed

work_zone pipeline · stage “exposure”

No new data was acquired for this phase. Exposure joins the work-zone inventory from phase 1 to the roadway metadata that already sits beside it, and reuses the hourly traffic profiles AVAIL already maintains for New York's federal MAP-21 submission.

1
Take the work extent, not the traffic jam

Each work zone knows both the segment the work is on and the segments downstream where congestion was attributed to it. Exposure reads the first and ignores the second. Including the downstream segments would count the queue a work zone causes as part of the work zone itself — a mistake that would have inflated the lane-mile-hours of a typical zone several-fold.

2
Decide how long it lasted

On the declared basis, with each occurrence capped so one never-closed record cannot carry a whole work zone, and with the duration-independent variant computed alongside every row. The basis, the cap and the number of capped occurrences are all recorded, so any figure can be recomputed on a different choice without re-deriving the dataset.

3
Turn a yearly average into an hourly volume

Annual average daily traffic is adjusted for the month and the day of the week, then spread across the day by one of twenty traffic distribution profiles — chosen by whether the segment is a freeway, how congested it is, and whether its peak is in the morning, the evening or neither. Weekends get their own profiles. Each active day of a work zone is walked separately, so a zone spanning a weekend is measured against weekend traffic.

4
Say what is missing

A measure with a missing input is published as unknown, never as zero, and each row carries flags for what was available: how many of its segments had a traffic count, how many had a length, which traffic figure was used, and whether the whole row is computable. Anything aggregating this dataset should filter on that last flag first.

5
Publish one vintage per year

As with the inventory, each calendar year is its own dataset vintage, computed against that year's roadway metadata — traffic counts change year to year, and using the wrong vintage's AADT would be a silent error. Re-running a year replaces it. The profile used, the duration basis and the metadata year are stamped on every run.

What NYSDOT needs to decide
  1. The duration basis, before the December 2026 policy deadline. It moves every exposure figure, and it is a methodology choice rather than a technical one.
  2. Lane-closure reporting. E1 and E2 exist only where a lane count was reported, and reporting varies from 92% of work zones in one Region to 25% in another. This is the single change that would most improve the measures.
  3. Whether hour-of-day matters enough to capture. If work-zone start and end times were recorded reliably, exposure could be measured against the traffic actually present rather than the daily average.
