Work Zone Speed Differential.

The safety surrogate. A work zone can pass the speed measure and still be the place where traffic drops 30 mph in a quarter mile — the taper, where rear-end crashes concentrate. This phase measures that drop two ways for every active hour of every zone: how much faster traffic was arriving than it was moving through, and how much slower the segment ran than its own normal. In CY2024 the arriving traffic was more than 15 mph faster than the zone in 6.0% of measured hours; the zone ran more than 15 mph under its own normal in 6.3%. Those are close, and they are mostly different hours.

What the measure says
Most hours, the approach is moving at the zone’s speed

The mean approach differential is +0.6 mph: arriving traffic is, on the typical active hour, barely faster than traffic in the zone. 63.6% of measured time sits within 5 mph either way. The hours that matter are the tail — 4.3% of the time over 15 mph, 0.7% over 25 — and they concentrate in 997 zones that are over the line for most of their hours.

The baseline drop is a different measure, not a check on this one

The zone ran +3.7 mph under its own cleaned normal on average, with 6.3% of hours more than 15 under. That number says how much the work slowed the road; the approach differential says how abruptly. A zone in a queue that has backed up past the approach scores high on the first and near zero on the second — the I-495 case below.

In 3.9% of hours the approach was slower than the zone

19,233 zone-hours had arriving traffic more than 15 mph slower than the zone. That is the queue: the back-up has passed the approach segments and the taper — the speed change — is somewhere further upstream, where the phase-5 queue length puts it. The differential at the queue tail is the refinement this leaves open; these hours count as measured, not over.

How the measure was computed

work_zone pipeline · stage “differential”

No new dataset. Phase 3 created wz_speed with five empty columns for this measure, because every view of a DAMA source shares one column list; this stage fills them for each vintage and stamps M4 on the view beside M1. The inputs are the ones every phase uses — the inventory, the active windows, the NPMRDS five-minute speeds and the phase-3 baseline — plus the corridor walk phase 5 built to find the queue.

1
Find the approach

For each zone's anchor segment, the 2 segments immediately upstream on the same corridor — same road, same linear, same direction, lower road order — by the same walk that found the queue. 35,562 of 41,036 zones with a CY2024 speed row have one; the rest are at a corridor's start, or on a road the network does not chain.

2
Measure both sides over the same minutes

Per active zone-hour with at least six of twelve five-minute periods observed on the anchor: the space-mean speed of the anchor and of the approach segments, over the identical active epochs. Space-means, so a slow short segment does not outvote a fast long one, and the same epochs, so the approach is not measured while the zone is not.

3
Two differentials, one line

Approach minus zone is the taper; the segment's cleaned twelve-month median at that hour and day-type minus zone is the drop from normal. Positive means the work zone slowed traffic. The flag is on the approach side only, over 15 mph — the rear-end surrogate the literature names — and the baseline drop is published beside it, not folded into it.

4
Roll up, then lay the evidence on the rows

M4 is the share of zone-hours over the line, hour-weighted and zone-mean, per tier, stamped on the view. Then the zone × hour-of-day cells already in wz_speed get the approach segments, the approach speed pooled the way speed_mean is, and the two differentials and the flag derived in SQL from the row's own speed and baseline — so no cell can disagree with the evidence beside it.

5
What it does not see

A taper further upstream than 2 segments, when the queue has passed the approach: 19,233 CY2024 zone-hours (3.9%) had an approach more than 15 mph slower than the zone. Those hours are measured and not over; the differential at the phase-5 queue tail is the refinement recorded for later. And the approach is measured where the network knows what upstream is: 41,143 of 279,234 cells have no approach speed and no flag.

What NYSDOT still needs to decide
  1. The line. 15 mph is the literature's number for a rear-end surrogate; 10 flags 18.7% of hours instead of 4.3%. The threshold is a parameter of the run and stamped on every view.
  2. Which differential to report. Declare the approach differential as M4 — it is the surrogate — and publish the baseline drop as the impact measure beside M1. Neither substitutes for the other.
  3. The queue tail. When the queue passes the approach the taper moves. Measuring the differential at the head of the phase-5 queue instead of at a fixed two segments would find it; it is a change to where the approach is, not to the arithmetic.
  4. Whether to pair it with crashes. Phase 7 located crashes on the approach segments; the zones that are over this line for most of their hours are the natural set to test the surrogate against.
