// executive summary

A reporting tool became a platform—and the platform changed what we know about the data.

In 2013 the Federal Highway Administration began publishing the National Performance Management Research Data Set (NPMRDS): probe travel times for every segment of the National Highway System, every five minutes. NYSDOT engaged the University at Albany’s AVAIL laboratory to make that data usable, and by 2021 the partnership had a working web tool for congestion and reliability reporting. This assignment asked a larger question: could that single tool become the state’s shared planning and performance data platform?

It could, and it did. TransportNY now carries three public products over one sign-in, one data layer, one map-authoring environment and one documentation set. NPMRDS answers how fast, how reliable and how congested a road is. TSMO answers how a region’s congestion, reliability, incidents and work zones looked over a year. The Freight Atlas is the interactive companion to the 2024 State Freight Plan. NYSDOT files its federal MAP-21 PM3 and HPMS submittals from the platform. The state’s metropolitan planning organizations use it to draft their transportation improvement programs and their congestion management processes.

The more consequential result is what building it taught us about the data underneath. Reliability and delay measures that the federal rule treats as settled turned out, on New York’s network, to be sensitive to things the rule does not mention: how many vehicles were actually observed, how long a segment is, which year’s map it was computed on, and how old the traffic count paired with it is. Much of the technical work of the last two years has been measuring those sensitivities, correcting what could be corrected, and publishing what could not be corrected as a stated limit rather than a silent one.

Where we are: all seven contract tasks are delivered or in final review. The three products were accepted and moved to production between August and September 2026; a 95-page documentation set is written and awaiting the owner’s review; the CY 2025 federal submittal was accepted in June 2026. Where we are going: the successor scope broadens PPDAF into an Integrated framework — IPPDAF — that adds land use and commuting, transit and modal accessibility, health, air quality and greenhouse-gas impacts, weather and resiliency, and engineering-grade speed and volume estimates, and that invites other NYSDOT research partners to build inside the same authentication and data ecosystem rather than beside it.

What we found

Five findings that changed how the platform computes or presents a number. Each is treated at length in Section 4.

88.1%
of probe records rest on four vehicles or fewer

NPMRDS labels every record by how many probes it averaged. In CY 2025, across 1.70 billion New York records, only 3.1 % reached the top band of ten or more. The federal reliability measures are computed on this.

3.4×
thin data makes a road look unreliable

AM-peak LOTTR crosses the federal 1.50 threshold on 38.2 % of segments with 50–249 observations but only 11.1 % of those with 2,000 +. Same network, same year. A large part of reported unreliability is a measurement artifact.

2.5×
congestion costs more than we were saying

The flat $20 per vehicle-hour was replaced in June 2026 with a class-weighted rate — $52 passenger, $42 single-unit truck, $77 combination truck — applied from each segment’s own traffic mix. The network blend is $50–55.

51%
of statewide delay is not recurrent

On the rebuilt CY 2025 series: 310.86M vehicle-hours of excessive delay statewide, 157.07M of it attributable to events — 46.84M to construction, 4.82M to accidents. Managing events is not a marginal lever.

1 in 5
incidents has a usable clearance time

Measured on all 3.7 million TRANSCOM events: all lanes reopened is present on 42.7 %, incident verified on 20.1 %, response on scene on 0.3 %. Traffic-incident-management performance cannot yet be measured from this feed.

36k→52k
the road network itself is not a fixed panel

The segment map is reissued every year and the data retroactively re-referenced. Segments grew from about 36,000 in 2017 to 52,473 in 2026. A route built on one year’s map can gain or lose segments on another, so a multi-year trend is never a clean before-and-after.

Introduction

1.1  The problem

A state department of transportation is asked, continuously, to answer questions of the form how is this road performing, compared to what, and is the thing we did about it working? Before probe data, answering one of those questions meant sending a car out to drive a corridor with a stopwatch, or standing up a tube count. The answer arrived weeks later, covered a few hours of a few days on a few miles, and could not be compared with an answer produced somewhere else by someone else.

The NPMRDS changed the supply side of that problem completely. It is continuous, statewide, federally funded, and methodologically identical from one year to the next. It did not, however, arrive usable. A month of New York data is tens of gigabytes of five-minute records keyed to a segment coding system most planners have never seen, on a network that is reissued every year, with coverage that varies by two orders of magnitude between an urban interstate and a rural arterial at three in the morning. Turning that into this corridor is 90 seconds slower in the PM peak than it was before the project, and here is how confident you should be in that is the actual work.

By 2021 NYSDOT and AVAIL had done that work for one class of question. The problem this assignment addresses is the next one: the Department does not have a travel-time problem, it has a data integration problem. Travel times are one dataset among dozens that planners, operations engineers, freight analysts and designers need to see against each other — counts, incidents, transit service, commodity flows, employment, crashes, culverts, bridges, capital projects. Each of those lived in a different system, on a different network, behind a different login, with a different answer to the question of who is allowed to see it.

1.2  Background: what the first phase left

The prior phase (2013–2021, reported in the NPMRDS Final Report of 31 December 2021) delivered the Web-based Congestion/Reliability Performance Measurement Platform: a PostgreSQL/PostGIS database on NYSDOT-funded servers at the University at Albany, a Node.js REST API, and a static JavaScript client with three interfaces — MACRO (a regional map), MICRO (customisable route reports), and a PM3 table — behind a four-tier authentication system. It established four things this assignment inherited and did not change:

  • Open source by agreement. NYSDOT and AVAIL settled early on third-party technologies with no licensing fees, explicitly so the Department would not be locked to one vendor in perpetuity.
  • A confederate design. Each research or consulting partner builds its own product against explicit needs, but commits to open source and to exposing its data and calculations through APIs, so a researcher at another institution can use a cached performance measure instead of recomputing it.
  • A community of practice. The NYSAMPO Modeling Working Group has served as the project’s technical panel since 2015 and is the most active of the NYSAMPO working groups. It is the reason the tools answer questions planners actually have.
  • Conflation on OpenStreetMap. The 2016–2017 Data Inventory and Integration Needs Assessment concluded that NYSDOT’s Linear Referencing System and Road Inventory System were the right spine for integration, and that conflating LRS/RIS and the TMC network to OSM was the way to keep every future dataset reachable.

It also left an explicit list of unfinished business. The incidents interface was “not fully functional and complete.” A new bottleneck ranking that separated recurrent from non-recurrent congestion was “in the process of being developed.” Traffic counts and transit level of service were being prototyped. And the substantive research questions the 2021 report named as future work — weather, incidents, freight, accessibility — had no home to be built in.

Two findings from the first phase deserve restating, because they set the pattern for everything in Section 4. Working the Mid-Hudson Valley congestion management process in 2018–2019, the project found that the federal PHED measure missed weekend and midday congestion that planners knew existed, and produced TED, the same computation without the peak-hour restriction. It also found that because excessive delay accumulates over a segment’s length, and TMC segments range from a tenth of a mile to several miles, ranking segments by total delay ranks them substantially by length — hence the per-mile normalisation. In both cases a federal measure was found to answer a slightly different question than the one being asked, and the response was to publish a second measure beside it rather than to argue with the rule. That is the method.

1.4  The NYSDOT practice this work touches

This is not a study that produced a recommendation. It is a platform that sits inside live departmental processes, and those processes constrain it. Five matter most:

  • Federal MAP-21 PM3 reporting. NYSDOT must report LOTTR, TTTR and, for urbanized areas over one million people, PHED, with targets and mid-period adjustments. AVAIL computes these and NYSDOT submits them. For the New York City urbanized area this requires agreement across NYSDOT, NJDOT, ConnDOT, PennDOT, NYMTC, NJTPA, DVRPC and the Western Connecticut Council of Governments — a multi-state reconciliation that has recurred every year of this assignment.
  • HPMS submittal. NYSDOT Traffic Data Services must file HPMS by mid-June each year; AVAIL appends the travel-time metric and runs the quality checks. The process starts in the spring and depends on the finalised TMC network arriving from the CATT Laboratory, usually in late March.
  • MPO congestion management processes. Federally required for transportation management areas; the platform is the data source for several. The measures the MPOs need (per-mile normalisation, weekend delay, a defensible coverage threshold) came out of that use and not out of the rule.
  • Project development and capital programming. Before-and-after studies, project prioritisation scores, speed and volume estimates for design. This is where the Department most wants numbers it can defend in a document, which is why Section 4’s findings about precision are consequential rather than academic.
  • Operations. Incident post-mortems, work-zone impact, regional performance reporting. This is the newest of the five in platform terms — the TSMO product did not exist at the start of the assignment — and the one where the data is least complete.

Research Method

A platform assignment has two methods running at once: a technical one for how the software and the numbers are made, and an institutional one for how the Department decides what to make. Both are described here, because several of the findings in Section 4 were produced by the institutional method — they surfaced because a planner in a weekly meeting said a number looked wrong.

2.1  A confederate, open-source architecture

The platform is a confederation, not a monolith. Every product reads through the same data layer and the same authentication, but each is a separately addressable application with its own release cadence. The rule that holds it together is that a capability added for one product becomes available to all of them: the Map Editor was built for the Freight Atlas and is now how every map on the platform is styled; the dataset catalog was built for NPMRDS and now serves freight downloads; the authentication system was built for NPMRDS in the prior phase and now covers the landing page, TSMO and the Freight Atlas on one session.

Three architectural decisions in this period are worth recording because they changed what was possible:

  • Consolidation onto one data manager (2025). NPMRDS, the Freight Atlas and the sandbox each had their own dataset catalog. They were merged into a single TransportNY Data Manager, with per-dataset and per-group permissions, so that a dataset uploaded once is visible to every product the uploader is entitled to publish into. This is the single change that made “one platform” true rather than aspirational.
  • Moving the travel-time processing to ClickHouse (late 2024). The five-minute archive outgrew the row-store. Column-oriented storage is why the Macro View can colour 52,000 segments for a year in a browser, and why the excessive-delay recompute of June 2026 was feasible at all.
  • Subdomain consolidation (2 September 2026). TSMO and the Freight Atlas moved onto the landing page’s origin so one sign-in carries across them. NPMRDS keeps its own address and its own session until it is ready to move — a deliberately partial migration, documented as such rather than hidden.

Everything is built on technologies with no licensing fee: PostgreSQL/PostGIS and ClickHouse for storage, Node.js for the API, React for the client, Python and Node for extract-transform-load, OpenStreetMap as the conflation spine, MapLibre for rendering. The code is the Department’s to take elsewhere.

2.2  The governance cadence

The project has met with NYSDOT weekly, without interruption, for the entire assignment. The minutes run from February 2020 to September 2026 and record roughly 185 meetings. That cadence is the method, not an overhead on it. Three patterns in it are worth naming for anyone designing a similar engagement:

  • A rolling agenda, not a status report. Items persist across weeks until they are closed. A question asked in one meeting is still on the page three weeks later with what has been learned appended. Several findings in Section 4 — the route-versus-API speed discrepancy, the overnight-epoch problem, the LOTTR anomalies before an HPMS submittal — took six to ten weeks of that to resolve and would not have survived a monthly check-in.
  • Rotation through subject meetings. From 2022 the month was structured: a technical team meeting, a TSMO meeting, a freight meeting, and a high-level project-management meeting that set priorities for the next month. When a subject needed more, it got its own series — road risk and resiliency in 2025, business points in 2026, running fixes from mid-2025.
  • A standing technical panel outside the Department. The NYSAMPO Modeling Working Group meets monthly and AVAIL has led it since 2015. It is where a feature meets users who do not report to the people who commissioned it. Presentations in this period covered the batch report tools, congestion management best practice, and the employment and establishments research.

Coordination under Task 4 extended well past NYSDOT: NYMTC and the Port Authority Freight Advisory Committee, the NYSAMPO Freight Working Group, the University at Buffalo IIMS team, TRANSCOM, Oak Ridge National Laboratory on the National Household Travel Survey, CPCS on the State Freight Plan data, ICF on the TSMO performance roadmap, the Eastern Transportation Coalition, and consultants including Parsons, Stantec and Dewberry working on Department projects.

2.4  Measure provenance: the dated change log

A platform whose numbers improve is a platform whose numbers change, and a number that changes without a record is a number nobody can cite. The discipline adopted in 2026 is that every change to a measure’s definition, parameter or underlying data is dated, is labelled by kind, and names the page that carries the detail. Four labels are used:

  • Methodology — a definition or parameter changed, and figures computed before and after are not comparable.
  • Data release — new months, or a recomputation, landed.
  • Map/TMC version — the segment network changed vintage.
  • Platform — a page or control changed and no number moved.

Every published figure names the data revision it was read at, and every page that holds a measure carries its own revision log which feeds the platform-wide one. Appendix D reproduces the log for the period covered by this report. The value of the practice is visible in Section 4.2: because the excessive-delay revision of 10 June 2026 is dated and its parameters are recorded, we can say without ambiguity that figures published before it do not compare with figures published after it — and anyone holding an older figure can find out why.

2.5  Development, QA and acceptance

Through 2024 the platform was developed and released in one place. In June 2025, at NYSDOT’s request and as the user base widened beyond the people who could absorb a broken page, the practice was formalised:

  • A development environment against production data. devtny.org runs the same data as transportny.org with the newest features and the newest bugs. Changes are exercised there and promoted after review.
  • A running-fixes document and a standing fixes meeting, separate from the weekly, so defects are triaged as defects and not as agenda items.
  • A public status page with per-feed data status and alerts, and a bug-report form on every page that writes to an issue-tracker dataset with its own dashboard. Reporting a problem stopped requiring knowing whom to email.
  • A formal design and acceptance review per product. Through 2026 each product went through a design review, an AVAIL quality pass and an NYSDOT acceptance review before going to production — TSMO and the Freight Atlas in August 2026, NPMRDS in September.

Two points of candour belong in a method section. First, error handling was identified by NYSDOT in June 2025 as a real weakness: users were seeing raw application errors. The response was two-part — prevent the error reaching the user, and forward it to developers automatically — and it is partly done. Second, the most common class of defect over five years was not a coding error but a silent upstream change: a segment renamed in the annual network, an authentication token flow altered at TRANSCOM, a federal file format revised without notice, a source withdrawn. The architecture response — automated ingestion with explicit checks, and a status page that shows what actually landed — is the right one and is not finished.

Products

Everything in this section is live software, in production, with users. Figures are captures of the running application taken in September 2026; each caption names the state that produced it so the figure can be reproduced or found to be stale.

3.1  TransportNY: one platform, three products

TransportNY — transportny.org — is the program. It began as an NYSDOT internal reporting tool and became a shared platform used by the Department to file its federal reports and by the state’s metropolitan planning organizations to draft their transportation improvement programs. Three products share one landing page, one sign-in and one documentation set, and a product switcher moves between them.

Everything that reads is public. Signing in is required to save a route or a report, and for administration: uploading data, building maps, managing users. That division was a deliberate 2025 decision and it changed the platform’s audience — the Freight Atlas in particular is now usable by anyone with the link, which is what makes it a companion to a published plan rather than an internal tool.

3.2  NPMRDS

The NPMRDS product turns probe travel times into measures for every segment of the monitored network, five minutes at a time since January 2017. It answers how fast, how reliable, how congested is this road, and how has that changed? It has four tools, and the division between them is the division between the two shapes every travel-time question takes: one road over time, or one moment across every road.

Reports is the first shape. A user draws a route — by clicking consecutive segments or by dropping markers and letting the network router connect them — saves it with validated tags, and charts it. Twelve templates in five question types cover the recurring questions: a snapshot of recent behaviour, seasonality, year-over-year, bi-directional comparison, before-and-after. A report holds one or more routes, each with its own date window, and one card per graph; it can be shared by its address or printed as a document. Two capabilities added in this period matter more than the rest: a route can be added to the same report twice with different date windows, which is what makes a before-and-after study a single artifact rather than two; and a batch report can run many routes against many periods at once, which is what made the Department’s speed-enforcement and capital-project evaluations practical at all.

The Macro View is the second shape: one measure, one year, every segment, on one map, with a worst-25 ranking panel and a download builder that exports the same filtered selection as CSV or GeoPackage with its method notes attached. It is the first step of a congestion management process — rank everything, then look closely at what ranks badly — and it is where the coverage measure lives, which is the control every other measure should be read against.

Seven measures are published: LOTTR, TTTR, TTTR₈₀, TED, PHED, percentile speed and data coverage. Three more are declared and deliberately withheld: free-flow speed, emissions and network attributes. They stay out of the measure menu so a user cannot select one and receive an empty map, but they remain listed in the tool’s own reference marked not yet computed. The rule behind that — an absent measure and a measure of zero are different claims, and nothing on the platform shows a zero for something it has not computed — is one of the small design commitments this project would most want carried forward.

The MAP-21 PM3 pages carry the federal submittal figures by year, with targets. They are the only pages on the platform to quote for a federal number, and Section 4.3 explains why that distinction had to be made structural rather than advisory. Route Comparison, a routes × periods matrix against a base period, is built and renders but is not published; it is the successor to the Batch Reports tool and the honest status is draft.

3.3  TSMO

TSMO — Transportation Systems Management and Operations — did not exist as a product when this assignment began. It was built from a 2021 prototype into four dashboards and two explorers, and it answers a different question than NPMRDS does: not how is this road but how did this region’s year go. Nothing in it requires an account.

  • Congestion — excessive delay split into what recurs every day and what events caused, by year, month, region and corridor, with its dollar cost.
  • Reliability — the federal PM3 reliability measures against their targets: Interstate and non-Interstate person-miles reliable, and truck travel-time reliability.
  • Incidents — delay attributed to reactive events, how long they last and clear, the landscape by category and month, and the events that hurt most.
  • Work Zones — delay attributed to construction, maintenance and emergency-operations events, where it lands and how long zones run.
  • Incident Search — every TRANSCOM event filtered to New York, found by region, county, period, category or keyword, opening onto an incident page that shows one event end to end: facts, response timeline, congestion footprint, corridors, congestion grid, map.
  • Corridor View — one corridor on one day as a time-space speed grid with the day’s events drawn over it. This is the tool operations staff recognise immediately, because it is the picture in their heads.

3.4  Freight Atlas

The Freight Atlas is the interactive companion to the 2024 New York State Freight Plan. It is one map with 39 switchable layers in 8 categories, a Maps Gallery that opens the plan’s own figures as ready-made views, a download catalog behind every layer, and the plan’s six goals and 20 strategies. Its home carries the plan’s headline numbers for the TRANSEARCH 2021 base year, and a stat strip: 39 map layers, 52 datasets, 1,145 mi of Primary Highway Freight System routes, 263 truck-parking sites, 37 bottlenecks, $304M of National Highway Freight Program funding for 2024–28.

Two things about it are worth more than the counts. First, the Atlas agrees with the plan by rule. When the home page’s mode-share chart was found to be deriving its shares live from the underlying data and landing a percentage point away from the published figure, the chart was changed to read the plan’s own numbers. A companion to a published document that disagrees with it in the third digit is worse than no companion. Second, the Atlas is authored, not programmed. Every layer is a saved map built in the Map Editor against a specific dataset version. Adding the 2025 truck bottlenecks was a data upload and a styling session, not a release.

Twenty-two of the plan’s 33 map figures are live as gallery tiles. The remaining eleven wait on data the Atlas does not hold, and the gallery says so rather than omitting them — the same rule as the Macro View’s unbuilt measures.

3.5  The data layer

Under all three products is one dataset catalog. A source has versions; a version has columns with descriptions, generated downloads in four formats, permissions by user and by group, and a provenance overview. Products read views of it; the API reads the same views; the Map Editor draws from the same versions. Merging the three separate catalogs into this one in 2025 is what made the platform coherent, and it is what allows a NYSDOT analyst to upload something into a private workspace and later publish it without asking a developer.

The catalog also carries the private half of the platform. Not every dataset NYSDOT holds may be redistributed — TRANSEARCH is under a non-disclosure agreement, oversize/overweight permit data and some safety data are restricted — and the readiness pipeline’s source data only state exists precisely so those can be present for processing without being publishable. Two dedicated catalog patterns exist, a general one and a freight-specific one mounted inside the Atlas.

3.6  Map Editor and Site Management

The Map Editor is the piece of this platform that most directly implements the Department’s stated interest in not depending on a vendor for routine work. An administrator creates a saved map, adds a layer from a dataset version, chooses a style type — simple, categorical, graduated, or interactive with viewer-switchable variants — sets bins by ck-means, quantile, equal interval, standard deviation or custom breaks, writes the legend labels by hand, configures the hover popup, and saves. A page picks up the saved map and publishes it.

Two details from that flow are worth recording because they are the kind of thing only production use surfaces. Road networks draw both directions on one line, so without a sideways offset only one direction’s colour is visible — a control that exists because a freight layer looked wrong. And a legend does not write itself: bin labels are raw numbers until an author edits them, which is why the editor insists the author can and the documentation insists the author should.

Site Management covers users, groups and access levels across every product, with per-dataset and per-page permissions, sign-up date and last-login visibility, and usage statistics. Beside it sit the public status page and the issue tracker described in §2.5.

3.7  Documentation

Task 6C requires documentation for all features of the framework, including a precise description of the methods and calculations. A 95-page documentation set was written in September 2026 covering getting started, the three products, the measures and datasets behind them, the APIs, and the administrator’s procedures. It is structured as five hubs — Get started, NPMRDS, TSMO, Freight Atlas, Measures & Data, Developers — plus an Admin section, a platform-wide change log and a glossary.

Its organising principle is worth stating because it is unusual and it is a deliverable in its own right: every measure page has the same anatomy — what it answers, how it is computed, the choices made in computing it, how much to trust it, where it appears on the platform, and its revision log. Every page names what it does not cover. Pages that document something not yet built say so and say what blocks it. A reader who wants to know whether a number is safe to put in a federal document can find that out in one page rather than by asking.

The set is written and is awaiting NYSDOT’s review; it is not yet published to the live site. That is the largest single open item in this assignment and is carried in §5.3.

3.8  APIs and bulk downloads

Subtask 3.2 commits to data integration through an API as a native function of the framework. Two programmatic surfaces are open, both on the data host:

  • The Data Manager API — the same read API every dashboard renders from. A caller sends a list of paths and receives a graph of values: a dataset’s length, its rows, its columns, filtered, grouped and ordered. Public reads need no token; a token from the sign-in endpoint lasts about six hours and carries whatever the account is entitled to.
  • The Batch Reports API — a single REST endpoint behind the batch tool. A caller posts routes as segment lists with time windows and column definitions and receives speed, travel time and hours of delay per route and period. It retires when Route Comparison ships, and the documentation says so.

Beside the APIs, every dataset version has a generated download in CSV, GeoJSON, Shapefile and GeoPackage, and the Macro View has its own export. What is not offered is stated as plainly: no OpenAPI document, no published rate limit, no bulk export of the raw five-minute table, no write access to platform data, and no client library other than a Python helper. Being specific about the absences is what makes the presences usable.

The API existed before it was documented, and that gap was a real cost — NYSDOT staff investigating business-intelligence tools in 2024 could not evaluate the platform as a source because there was nothing to read. The reference was written in 2025–26 and now includes worked examples and a changelog.

Findings

Nine findings, ordered from the one that affects the most published numbers to the ones that open new capability. The first five are about measurement and are the substantive research result of this assignment. Every figure names where it came from.

4.1  The probe data is thinner than the measures assume

The federal reliability measures treat a segment’s travel-time distribution as a distribution. On New York’s network, for most segments most of the time, it is a handful of observations. NPMRDS labels every five-minute record by how many probes it averaged — A for one to four, B for five to nine, C for ten or more. Across 1,695,358,623 New York records for CY 2025:

  • 88.1 % are band A — four observed vehicles or fewer.
  • 8.8 % are band B.
  • 3.1 % reach band C.
  • 59.6 % of records carry no truck data at all, which is the population the truck reliability measure is computed from.

Thin data is not merely imprecise; on these measures it is biased in a known direction. LOTTR is a ratio of the 80th percentile to the 50th. With few observations the 80th percentile is estimated from the tail, and a tail estimated from four numbers is far more likely to be extreme than one estimated from two thousand. The consequence is measurable on our own data: AM-peak LOTTR crosses the federal 1.50 threshold on 38.2 % of segments with 50–249 observations but only 11.1 % of segments with 2,000 or more — a 3.4× difference on the same network in the same year.

Segment length runs the same way and harder, because short segments both accumulate fewer probes and are more sensitive to a single slow traversal. 56.0 % of segments under a tenth of a mile are flagged unreliable against 7.3 % of segments over a mile — 7.7×. Segments under a tenth of a mile are about a third of New York’s segment count and about 1.5 % of its mileage. They are, disproportionately, intersection stubs and bridge approaches.

Coverage also moves over time in a way that defeats naive year-to-year comparison. The platform now recognises nine coverage eras. Across the published years, the share of five-minute bins that report at all runs from 36.3 % to 51.5 % for all-vehicle data and from 8.0 % to 23.5 % for truck data. Overnight is thin everywhere, and no record below 2 mph reaches the platform — the feed arrives filtered upstream.

What was done about it. Three things, none of which is a correction to the federal measure, because the federal measure is not ours to correct. First, data coverage was published as a measure in its own right in August 2026, so a user can colour the map by how much of the underlying data actually arrived before reading any other measure on it. Second, measured precision bands were added to every measure in the August 2026 methodology rewrite, so a figure travels with an honest statement of how much of it is signal. Third, the documentation states the limit at the point of use: read months, not days, on secondary arterials and rural roads.

What follows for the Department. Two operational implications, and one for the next scope. Operationally: a data-coverage floor should be applied before ranking segments in a congestion management process — the Mid-Hudson Valley work settled on 10 % of peak-period epochs, and published practice elsewhere uses roughly 19 % of a day — and a very short segment’s reliability figure should not be reported without its length beside it. For the next scope: a segment-level confidence flag on the published PM3 series, so that a bad LOTTR on a thinly observed stub is distinguishable at a glance from a bad LOTTR on a well-observed interstate.

4.2  The delay measures had to be rebuilt

The platform’s excessive-delay series — the number behind every congestion figure in TSMO and behind the cost of congestion — was rebuilt in June 2026. The rebuild changed three things at once, and figures published before 10 June 2026 do not compare with figures published after it. That statement is in the change log, on the dashboard, and in the documentation, because a silent revision of a headline number is the most damaging thing a platform like this can do.

  • The threshold. Excessive delay now accumulates below the greater of 20 mph or 60 % of the posted speed limit, with posted limits read live per segment rather than from a static table. This is the federal definition applied with better inputs.
  • The baseline. Attribution now compares against the median rather than the mean. A mean baseline on a distribution with a long congested tail is itself partly congestion, which made the recurrent share drift upward.
  • The cap. Attribution is capped so that an event cannot be assigned more delay than actually exists on the segment. Uncapped attribution had been producing individual events credited with implausible totals.

The series was restarted and refilled from the newest complete month backwards. Its current extent is honest about its gaps: 2018 and 2021–2025 are computed; 2019 and 2020 are not; 2017 has no rows. The gap is displayed as a gap rather than as zeros.

The headline result. On the rebuilt series, CY 2025, statewide, all vehicles: 310.86 million vehicle-hours of excessive delay, of which 157.07 million (51 %) is attributed to events rather than to recurrent congestion. Construction accounts for 46.84 million and accidents for 4.82 million. That non-recurrent share is the single most policy-relevant number this assignment produced: it says that on New York’s network, managing events is not a marginal lever beside capacity — it is half the problem.

The cost of that delay was also wrong, and by a large factor. The platform had been valuing a vehicle-hour at a flat $20 and separately double-counting occupancy. In June 2026 both were replaced with a class-weighted value of time applied from each segment’s own traffic mix: $52 per passenger-vehicle hour, $42 per single-unit-truck hour, $77 per combination-truck hour. The network blend is roughly $50–55 per vehicle-hour, about 2.5× the rate it replaced. Every dollar figure recomputed on the new basis rises by that factor. Because the recomputation of the published series was still in progress at the time of writing, pages show which basis they are on — the alternative, showing a mixture without saying so, was rejected.

A fourth parameter, the free-flow reference, turned out to matter less than expected — but only because it was measured. The delay threshold needs an uncongested reference, conventionally the 85th-percentile off-peak speed (equivalently, the 15th-percentile travel time). Computing that within each year makes a year’s delay figure partly a function of that year’s congestion. An anchored variant, which fixes the reference window across years, was published in August 2026 beside the own-year variant rather than replacing it. The anchor window moved twice as the publish scope grew, settling at June 2023 – July 2024. On identical CY 2025 data the anchored variant yields 1.18 % more network delay than the own-year reference. Publishing both, with the difference quantified, is worth more than choosing one.

The pattern in §4.1 and §4.2, stated once

Every one of these corrections was found the same way: a figure was published, a practitioner said it did not match what they knew about the road, and the weekly meeting held the question open long enough to find out who was right. The Mid-Hudson Valley planners who produced TED in the previous phase, the reader who mistook a grey legend band for non-recurrent delay in August 2026, the engineer who noticed a truck-reliability threshold that flagged three quarters of the network — all the same mechanism. A platform with a community of practice attached finds its own errors; one without one does not.

4.3  Federal and analytical had to be separated structurally

The measures in §4.2 improve. The measures NYSDOT submits to FHWA must not, because a submittal must stay compliant and reproducible against the rule as written and against the figures already filed. For most of this assignment the same code produced both, which meant every methodological improvement was also a compliance risk, and the safe response — do not improve anything during the reporting window — was quietly throttling the research.

On 14 August 2026 the analytical series was forked from the MAP-21 computation. The federal computation is now frozen. The analytical series can change its definitions freely, and every subsequent methodology change applies to it alone. No number moved on the day of the fork.

The fork is a software change with an editorial consequence that runs through the whole platform. Two measure families now share the same names — LOTTR and TTTR exist in a federal form and an analytical form — so the platform has to say, at every point of use, which one a reader is looking at. It does: the MAP-21 PM3 pages are declared the only pages to quote for a federal number; the Macro View and the TSMO Reliability dashboard are declared analytical; a dedicated documentation page exists whose entire job is that distinction; and the measure index marks each appearance federal or analytical.

Immediately after the fork the analytical series gained what the federal one cannot have: a truck reliability index at the 80th percentile rather than the 95th (TTTR₈₀, chosen because the sample can support it — see §4.1), per-row quality columns, per-stream era tags, delay-threshold diagnostics, precision bands, and the anchored delay variant. We recommend this separation as general practice for any agency computing federal measures from a platform it also uses for analysis. Without it, either compliance or research gives way, and it is usually research.

A concrete, and instructive, example of what the analytical side can now fix quickly: in August 2026 the Macro View’s TTTR colour threshold was found to be set at 1.50 when the Interstate target is 2.00. Correcting it moved the share of segments shown above threshold from 74.1 % to 44.7 % with no change whatever to the computation. A display parameter had been telling planners that three quarters of the freight network failed a standard it actually met.

4.4  The network is not a fixed panel, and the volumes are two years stale

Every longitudinal analysis on this data assumes a stable unit of observation. There isn’t one. The TMC segment map is reissued annually — typically in the autumn — and the current year’s data is retroactively re-referenced to the new map. Codes, lengths and end points move. The network has grown from roughly 36,000 segments in 2017 to 46,000 in 2019 to 52,029 (2024), 52,157 (2025) and 52,473 (2026).

The practical consequences are constant and unglamorous. A saved route built on one year’s map can lose or gain segments when run for another, which is a support question the project answered many times before the tooling was changed to re-reference routes to the year-appropriate segments automatically. A per-segment series across years is not a panel. And the annual reissue means that travel times reported early in a year are not the final reported travel times — a trap the prior-phase report also flagged, and which has not gone away.

Volumes are a second, independent time-base problem. Delay is a travel-time measure multiplied by a volume, and the volume is annual average daily traffic that lags the travel times by about two years. A delay trend is therefore partly an AADT revision history. This is named in the documentation as one of two explicit traps for readers comparing across years. The conflation to RIS was undertaken in large part to replace derived AADT with New York’s own counts wherever they exist, and that work — the annual LRS/RIS ↔ OSM ↔ TMC conflation, automated in 2025 — is the most valuable piece of unglamorous infrastructure in the project. It is the join through which every non-probe dataset reaches the travel-time network.

One further caution, recorded here because it recurred: two different segment counts circulate and both are correct. The TSMO methodology page counts 54,249 segments in its network table against 52,473 on the 2026 map, because the table accumulates every segment that has ever carried data while the map is one year’s. Any comparison of counts must say which basis it is on.

The dashboard counts changed; the underlying store did not. Category schemes inherited from an operational feed do not automatically make analytical categories, and the cost of assuming they do is a set of figures that quietly overlap.

4.6  Freight: the atlas must agree with the plan, and the bottleneck method had to be reconciled

Two findings from the freight work generalise beyond freight.

An interactive companion to a published plan has to agree with it, digit for digit. The Freight Atlas home derived its mode-share chart live from the underlying commodity data and produced numbers a little different from the plan’s published figures. Both were defensible; having both was not. The chart now reads the plan’s own figures, and the rule — the Atlas agrees with the plan, and a layer’s values are its dataset’s at the dataset’s stated vintage — is written down. The general principle: a live platform and a static document covering the same subject need an explicit precedence rule, decided once, not per chart.

A measure that already exists inside an agency has to be reconciled, not reinvented. NYSDOT had a freight-bottleneck methodology. AVAIL implemented one. Through late 2024 and into 2025 the two were run side by side and the differences chased down: AVAIL’s typically produced more segments, with urban and suburban results close and the divergence concentrated in one or two segments per corridor. Adding a three-year lookback to exclude construction-driven false positives brought them together. The reconciliation took months and was worth every week of it, because a bottleneck list that disagrees with the Department’s own list is not adoptable no matter which is better.

Beyond those, the freight work produced the substantive integration this assignment is most visibly judged on: TRANSEARCH commodity flows assigned to segments with tonnage and value totals and a hover that reads them; the National Highway Freight Network, the state Freight Core Highway Network and the National Multimodal Freight Network compared and clipped consistently to New York with the critical urban and rural corridors labelled; rail by class, maritime, pipelines, intermodal facilities and airports; truck parking including undesignated clusters; and truck crash and freight-demand-intensity layers. Fifty-two datasets, each with a download and a vintage.

4.7  Employment: no single establishment dataset is right, and the disagreement is the finding

Where do people work? is the question behind freight generation, accessibility, origin–destination estimation and commuting analysis, and NYSDOT asked it directly in 2026. The obvious answer — a commercial establishments file with a point per business — is not reliable enough on its own to carry a planning conclusion. Through the first half of 2026 the project compared four sources against each other at every geography from state to census block: commercial business points, the Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages, LEHD LODES workplace area characteristics, and County Business Patterns.

The comparison is the finding. Totals can agree closely at county level while individual industry codes diverge severely — Erie County matched well in aggregate and badly by two-digit NAICS — and at sub-county geographies the spread between sources can be more than two to one. The response was not to pick a winner but to build a best-estimate employment layer with the confidence level and every contributing raw source exposed on hover, at MPO, county, tract, block group and block. A planner sees the estimate, how much to trust it, and what it was made from. A user who spots a wrong establishment can flag it back to the vendor.

This work is the foundation for several items in Section 6 and is the clearest example in the project of the platform being used for research rather than reporting: four datasets loaded, compared, and published as a reconciled product with its uncertainty attached.

4.8  Resiliency: criticality is computable from the network the platform already has

Road risk and resiliency was scoped with NYSDOT through 2025 and produced a working method. The finding is that a usable criticality measure falls out of the conflated network at no extra data cost, and the expensive half is exposure, not importance.

The composite is Resiliency Score = Risk × Criticality, normalised. Criticality combines two graph properties of the road network: betweenness, which behaves as a proxy for accessibility and for the Department’s existing corridor importance factor, and redundancy — the extra travel time incurred when a segment is removed and traffic must route around it — weighted by AADT to give a consequence. Both are computed from the routable conflation network the platform maintains anyway. Risk is scored from floodplain (100-year, 500-year), observed TRANSCOM flooding and washout events, stream crossings and culverts, drawing on the National Hydrography Dataset, NYSDOT’s large-culvert inventory, regional small-culvert data and DEC culvert data. The culvert and crossing inventories are the incomplete part.

The same routing machinery produced a second, immediately useful capability in 2026: a bridge detour tool that, for any bridge, computes the detour in each direction over the segment graph, honouring turn restrictions and one-way streets and using speed limits for travel time, and reports the added seconds and added distance. It was built to a live NYSDOT request and delivered as a geodatabase alongside the interactive prototype. It is explicitly not a traffic-diversion model — it does not estimate how much volume shifts or what that does to the alternate route — and that boundary is stated rather than blurred.

4.9  Institutional findings

Four findings that are about how the work got done rather than about the data. They are offered because they are transferable, and because an agency commissioning a similar platform will otherwise learn them the same slow way.

  • Authoring beats bespoke development, and the difference compounds. Every capability moved from code into an authoring tool — maps into the Map Editor, pages into the page editor, datasets into the Data Manager, report layouts into templates — removed a standing dependency on AVAIL and shortened the loop between a question and an answer from a release cycle to an afternoon. The Freight Atlas is the proof: 39 layers, none of them a component someone wrote.
  • The weekly meeting is the instrument. Roughly 185 of them. Nearly every finding in this section entered through one. The rolling agenda that keeps an unresolved question visible for weeks is the specific mechanism, and it is cheap.
  • Adoption is gated on trust, and trust is gated on documentation. The platform had working tools well before it had working documentation, and the documentation gap was the binding constraint on rollout to the regions, on consultant use, and on business-intelligence integration. The 2026 documentation effort is a delayed payment on that.
  • State the absences. The measures that are declared but not computed, the two years missing from the delay series, the eleven plan maps not yet drawn, the timestamps that are not there, the API features that do not exist — every one of these is published as an explicit absence. It is the single practice that most raised the platform’s credibility with technical users, and it costs nothing but nerve.

A candid accounting of the assignment as it closes on 30 September 2026: what each task delivered, who is actually using the result, what is not finished, and what we recommend about servers.

Task 1 — Quarterly Reporting. Delivered every quarter of the assignment in the required one-page Microsoft Word format. In mid-2026 the process was tightened at NYSDOT’s request so the quarterly report and its invoice arrive together within a week of the quarter closing.

Task 2 — Integration of Specified Datasets/Applications. Delivered. Of the thirteen dataset families named in the scope, nine are integrated and in production — NPMRDS, the LRS/RIS conflation, Traffic Data Services counts, MAP-21 PM3 and HPMS, Census demographic and commutation products, the statewide GTFS, TRANSCOM archived speed and incident data, freight data (TRANSEARCH, the Freight Analysis Framework, the state networks), and the establishments data that underlies the employment work. 5.2  Adoption and use

The platform is used, and by more kinds of people than at the start of the assignment. In rough order of volume:

  • NYSDOT Policy, Planning and Performance — the federal submittals, the statewide performance picture, project evaluation.
  • The state’s metropolitan planning organizations — congestion management processes, transportation improvement programs, corridor studies. Access is by agency group.
  • NYSDOT regions and operations — the TSMO dashboards and Corridor View. This is the newest constituency; a presentation series to the regional transportation engineers began in Rochester in November 2024.
  • Other agencies and authorities — the MTA (which arrived in numbers within days of being given access in November 2025), NYMTC, and the freight advisory committees.
  • Authorised consultants working on NYSDOT projects — Parsons on a 787-versus-Twin-Bridges reliability question, Stantec on the Rochester Inner Loop, ICF on operations analysis.

Three concrete uses from the last two years illustrate the range better than a user count would. A Hudson Valley planner used a route report to measure the effect of reducing an arterial from three lanes to two for ten days, and found the travel-time increase real but below the threshold that would register as hours of delay — a finding the tool made cheap and which would previously have cost a field study. The Department used batch reports to evaluate speed-enforcement deployments across many like-kind segments and dissimilar time frames in one comparison. And the Freight Atlas launched alongside the 2024 State Freight Plan as its public companion, which is the first time a product of this project has been aimed past the professional audience.

We would not claim adoption is complete. Use is concentrated in the planning constituency that has been part of the community of practice since 2015. Regional and operations use is growing from a low base and depends on outreach the Department leads. And the honest limiting factor named in §4.9 — documentation — has only just been addressed.

5.4  Servers and hosting

Task 7 requires a recommendation on NYSDOT’s server needs for hosting and visualising datasets. The current arrangement — NYSDOT-funded hardware sited at the University at Albany and operated jointly by AVAIL and University Research IT — has served the project since 2017 and has been a good arrangement: it puts the machines next to the people who tune the queries, and it has kept the cost of a very large archive low. The original hardware reached end of life during this assignment and was dispositioned through the University’s e-recycling process in August 2026; the documentation of that disposal was requested and provided.

Our recommendation for the successor assignment is a hybrid, not a wholesale move to the cloud, for three reasons the project has evidence for. The travel-time archive is the single largest cost driver and it is read-heavy, latency-sensitive and analytically hot — exactly the profile where owned columnar hardware is dramatically cheaper than metered cloud storage and compute. The public-facing tier, by contrast, is bursty and benefits from managed redundancy, which NYSDOT itself raised in June 2025 when discussing uptime. And the platform now has obligations it did not have in 2021: a published status page implies a service level, and a federal submittal pipeline implies a recovery plan.

Specifically, we recommend the successor scope fund: (1) replacement analytic hardware sized for the archive, retained in the current arrangement; (2) a managed, redundant tier for the public-facing applications and the API, so an outage is degraded service rather than an outage; (3) an explicit, tested backup and recovery procedure for the derived series and the Data Manager, distinct from the raw archive which can be re-acquired; and (4) a written statement of the intended service level, so that the status page can be measured against something.

Where the project is headed

This section is drawn from the 2026 extension scope of work, in negotiation as this report is written. Sections 6.1–6.4 describe what that scope commits to. Section 6.5 is AVAIL’s recommendation on sequencing and is offered as advice, not as a statement of what has been agreed.

6.1  From PPDAF to IPPDAF

The successor scope adds one word and a great deal of consequence: Integrated Planning and Performance Data and Analytics Framework. The seven-task structure is unchanged. What changes is that the framework stops being a platform that AVAIL extends and starts being an architecture that other parties build inside. In the scope’s own terms, AVAIL is to provide integration partners with “a system of rules, recommendations and interoperability guidelines” covering data storage, cybersecurity and analytical capability, such that the final deployment is “a unified system of data sources, APIs and user interfaces which can be accessed centrally but hosted in a decentralized manner,” published through a GitHub repository serving as the code hub for NYSDOT’s open-source resources.

The needs the framework is to meet are broader than travel time: land use, employment and commuting patterns; transit and modal demand and accessibility; TSMO performance; health and air quality impacts; greenhouse-gas impacts; weather-induced operational issues; and engineering-grade highway speed and volume estimates.

6.2  Integration (Task 2)

The continuing commitments are the recurring deliverables of §3.9: monthly NPMRDS updates with a documented API, annual LRS conflation with milepoint-level data by shapefile and API, annual counts integration, and the annual PM3 and HPMS production including the cross-state urbanized-area coordination. The new commitments are where the work grows:

  • Census and commutation — a custom API for the American Community Survey driving maps, graphs and dashboards, plus advice on CTPP, LODES and commercial origin–destination products in project planning and regional modelling.
  • Application integration — hosting the authentication system and onboarding other NYSDOT applications: the CUNY Employer Data Viewer, the 511NY GTFS manager and Open Trip Planner, the C2SMART work-zone and capital-program tools, transit accessibility tools, safety data (ALIS/CLEAR), and state hazard-mitigation tools.
  • Modelling and forecasting — reporting on activity-based models (the New York Best Practices Model, Replica, ActivitySim, MATSim-NY) and investigating activity-based origin–destination tables as inputs to scenarios in the platform’s own tools.
  • TRANSCOM and real-time data — continuing the incident work, conflating the HERE network to TMC and LRS, and, if NYSDOT provides API access, real-time performance measures through the platform API. AVAIL holds an archived HERE sample (October 2021 to June 2022, about 335 million rows on roughly 27,000 segments, polled every two minutes) and can consume the full history or turn on the live feed if the Department wants it.
  • Freight, NHTS, the NYMTC Transportation Information Gateway, and weather and resiliency — each with a specific deliverable named in the scope.
  • Integration guidance for the sources not yet reachable: DMV vehicle registration and fuel type, third-party providers, cloud datasets, the NYC connected-vehicle pilot, the Joint Traffic Management Center, and the Region 1 ActiveITS pilot.

6.3  Customization (Task 3)

Eleven customization areas are named. Several are direct continuations of findings in Section 4 and are the ones we would weight most heavily:

  • Commutation pattern analysis — origins and destinations for a highway segment, built from disaggregation of CTPP and NHTS to parcels using land use, LODES and business location data, with dynamic routing and trip allocation on historical NPMRDS speeds. This is the natural continuation of §4.7.
  • Corridor and project-area profile tools — prototyping data processes that directly support project development, so engineers and planners spend less effort collecting and formatting data for capital-project proposals.
  • Incident post-mortem and near-real-time analysis — operations dashboards on up-to-date data, and incorporation of incident response data when it becomes available. This depends on §4.5.
  • Freight trip generation at parcel level from establishments data, following NCFRP 25, with alternative-fuel truck location and operations analysis.
  • Custom calculations — cost–benefit, project prioritisation, economic benefit, network AADT modelling, congestion cost and emissions.
  • Modal accessibility and detour analysis, and TSMO integration for incident and ITS management.
  • Machine learning — graph attention networks to model future speeds from the current network, and to estimate volume from probe data by training against continuous count locations. Explicitly experimental, with a potential collaboration with NYU on incident impact modelling downstate.

6.4  Coordination, production and user resources (Tasks 4–6)

Coordination widens to the MPO association, partner state agencies (Empire State Development, the Department of Labor, DEC, NYSERDA), TRANSCOM, NITTEC for Buffalo TSMO performance, third-party data providers, Oak Ridge, C2SMART, NYSDOT’s mobility-services consultants and membership organisations such as the Eastern Transportation Coalition. Task 5 continues as a quick-turnaround service. Task 6 keeps its four sub-tasks and adds emphasis on use-case reports, performance-measurement methodology guidance, replicable use-case software templates, and a formal research protocol approved by the project manager before research on end-users commences.

Conclusions

One. The platform objective was met. A single-purpose NPMRDS reporting tool became TransportNY: three public products over one sign-in, one dataset catalog, one map-authoring environment and one documentation set, producing New York’s federal performance submittals and serving the Department, its regions, fourteen metropolitan planning organizations, partner authorities, consultants and — through the Freight Atlas — the public. The confederate, open-source architecture the first phase chose held up under five years of load and three products.

Two. The most important research result is about measurement, not about traffic. The federal reliability and delay measures, applied to New York’s network, are more sensitive to observation count, segment length, network vintage and volume vintage than their definitions acknowledge. 88.1 % of the probe records they rest on average four vehicles or fewer, and thin observation inflates apparent unreliability by a factor of 3.4. This does not make the measures wrong; it makes them measurements with error bars that nobody was drawing. The platform now draws them.

Three. Where a measure could be improved without breaking compliance, it was, and the improvements were substantial. The excessive-delay series was rebuilt on a corrected threshold, a median baseline and a capped attribution. The value of time was replaced with a class-weighted rate roughly 2.5 times the flat rate it superseded. An anchored free-flow reference was published beside the own-year one with the difference quantified at 1.18 % of network delay. A display threshold error was found that had been reporting three quarters of the freight network as failing a standard it met.

Four. On the rebuilt series, 51 % of New York’s statewide excessive delay in CY 2025 was attributable to events rather than to recurrent congestion — 157.07 of 310.86 million vehicle-hours, with construction the largest single attributed cause at 46.84 million. For a Department weighing operations investment against capacity investment, that is the number in this report with the most direct policy content.

Five. Federal reporting and analytical improvement are incompatible in one code path, and separating them structurally — done 14 August 2026 — is a practice we recommend to any agency in the same position. Before the fork, every methodological improvement was a compliance risk, and the safe response was to improve nothing during the reporting window.

Six. The binding constraint on the platform’s value is no longer capability; it is documentation, onboarding and data completeness at the source. Traffic-incident-management measures are unavailable not because they are hard to compute but because two timestamps are missing from four fifths of events. Regional adoption lagged not for want of tools but for want of guidance. Both are addressable and neither is a software problem.

Seven. Moving capability out of code and into authoring tools — maps, pages, datasets, report templates — was the highest-leverage architectural decision of the assignment. Thirty-nine Freight Atlas layers exist without a line of layer-specific code. Every such move removed a standing dependency on the research team and shortened the loop between a question and an answer from a release cycle to an afternoon.

Eight. The governance cadence is not overhead on the research; it is the instrument that produced it. Roughly 185 weekly meetings over the assignment, a rolling agenda that keeps an unresolved question visible for weeks, and a monthly technical panel of practitioners who do not report to the sponsor. Nearly every finding in Section 4 entered through that channel, usually as a planner saying a number did not match a road they knew.

Suggestions for additional research

Four questions this assignment opened and did not close. Segment-level confidence: can a published PM3 series carry a per-segment reliability-of-the-reliability-measure flag that a non-specialist can act on? Volume from probes: can a network trained on continuous count locations estimate volume where counts do not exist, and close the two-year AADT lag? Diversion: what is the minimum viable model that turns the detour capability of §4.8 into an estimate of how much traffic actually shifts, and what it costs the alternate route? Field data as an intervention: what is the smallest change to incident reporting practice that would make traffic-incident-management performance measurable statewide, and what would it be worth?

Statement on Implementation

Most of this project is already in practice: the products are in production and the federal submittals come out of them. What follows is what would need to occur for the findings to change practice, and how the results transfer.

8.1  Introducing the findings into practice

  • Adopt a coverage floor as departmental guidance. Section 4.1 shows that ranking segments without a data-coverage threshold ranks measurement error. A stated floor — and a stated practice of reporting a short segment’s length beside its reliability figure — would make congestion management processes across the fourteen MPOs comparable with each other. This needs an NYSDOT position, not more analysis.
  • Publish the documentation. Ninety-five pages are written and awaiting review. Until they are live, the platform’s measures are only as trustworthy as the person explaining them, which does not scale past the people already in the weekly meeting.
  • Decide the emission-rate table. One decision unblocks an entire measure family and, with it, the health, air-quality and greenhouse-gas analysis the successor scope names.
  • Treat the incident timestamp gap as a field-operations item. Section 4.5 quantifies it. Acting on it belongs with the people who enter the data, and the measurement is now available to make that case.
  • Carry the change-log discipline forward contractually. The dated methodology log, the stated absences, the basis named on every figure — these are practices, not features, and practices lapse. Naming them in the successor scope is how they survive a change of staff.

8.2  Technology transfer

Transfer has been continuous rather than terminal, which is the appropriate shape for a platform assignment. The channels used, and recommended for continuation:

  • The NYSAMPO Modeling Working Group, monthly, AVAIL-facilitated since 2015. Presentations in this period covered the batch report tools, congestion management best practice, and the employment and establishments research. A standing platform agenda item was proposed in 2026 and should be adopted.
  • Regional and operations briefings — the regional transportation engineers series beginning in Rochester in November 2024, and the ITS coordinators meetings.
  • Freight fora — NYMTC’s Port Authority Freight Advisory Committee, the NYSAMPO Freight Working Group, and the multi-state MAP Forum.
  • Written and web-based guidance — the 95-page documentation set, the API reference with worked examples, and the map-authoring and dataset-upload procedures that let NYSDOT staff operate the platform without AVAIL.
  • Academic and professional dissemination — the Rutgers CAIT webinar series and Transportation Research Board activity, and the graduate practicum and internship pipeline through the University at Albany’s planning programs, which has been this project’s principal mechanism for training people who later work in the field.
  • Open source — the code is unencumbered and the successor scope commits to a public repository as NYSDOT’s open-source hub. This is the transfer mechanism with the longest reach and the one most worth completing.

Two additions we would recommend for the successor assignment: short video guidance for the ten or so tasks that account for most use, which the scope calls for and which written documentation does not substitute for; and quarterly user webinars with a published use case each time, which NYSDOT proposed in June 2025 as the shape of post-launch engagement and which remains the right idea.
