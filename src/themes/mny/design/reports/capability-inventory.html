The verdict

Broad across the state.
Shallow within most towns.

Combined, the seven county exports carry 2,354 capability rows across 169 jurisdictions. The vocabulary is largely under control — 261 raw Capability labels fold to 252 once case is normalized — but that control masks a bigger gap: the single most common label in the whole dataset isn’t a real capability at all. It’s “Other,” used 107 times as a write-in catch-all.

Coverage is heavily weighted toward Planning and Regulatory (1,062 rows, 45% of the dataset) — plans, codes, and ordinances that every jurisdiction was likely asked about directly. Asset capabilities, by contrast, appear on just 3 of 2,354 rows. That is almost certainly not because these counties own no public assets worth tracking — it reads as a category the intake process rarely reached.

The deeper problem isn’t the vocabulary, though — it’s how unevenly the record runs across jurisdictions. One town’s capability table can run 26 rows deep across every domain; another’s is a single line. Both look identical in a simple row count, but they mean very different things.

Of the 21 single-domain jurisdictions, 16 report Planning & Regulatory and nothing else — for those towns, the entire capability record is one line: adoption of the county Hazard Mitigation Plan. A count of 1 is genuinely ambiguous here: it could mean the jurisdiction truly has no other capability worth logging, or that the intake process never asked past the plan adoption. This inventory can’t tell those two apart — see the Capabilities vs. Capacity report for why that distinction matters.

Read the Schenectady row carefully. Its lead in rows-per-jurisdiction and average breadth is driven substantially by a single 60-row “Countywide” entry — not by its 9 towns individually out-reporting Allegany’s 48. Before treating breadth as a capability signal across counties, confirm whether each county reported at the town level or rolled entries up county-wide; right now those two reporting styles are mixed together in the same table.

“Other” outranks every real category. It shows up as a write-in catch-all under Administrative and Technical (50 rows), Planning and Regulatory (47), and Education and Outreach (10). This is the same normalization gap a prior pass over this dataset already mapped — that pass reduced the vocabulary to roughly 104 standardized values. The fix is coding these 107 write-ins into specific values, not adding more open-text room.

Recommended path

Normalize before you scale to more counties

1 · Fold case duplicates

261 → 252 labels in one mechanical pass — cheap, and it doesn’t require touching a single row’s meaning.

2 · Code the “Other” rows

107 rows across 3 capability types — almost certainly land on values already in the normalized list from the earlier pass.

3 · Fix the reporting unit

Decide once whether county-level or per-jurisdiction rows are the standard unit, so breadth comparisons across counties mean the same thing.

Net effect: a cleaner 250-value vocabulary, no “Other” catch-all left standing, and county comparisons that reflect actual capability differences instead of reporting-style artifacts. None of this closes the bigger gap, though — see the Capabilities vs. Capacity report for what this inventory still can’t tell you.
