The verdict

A checked box isn’t evidence
of a working capacity.

Capability is a discrete resource, tool, plan, staff role, or funding mechanism a jurisdiction possesses — the inventory item. Capacity is the jurisdiction’s actual functional ability to leverage that resource: staffing depth, funding adequacy, technical expertise, political will, public support. A jurisdiction can check “Hazard Mitigation Plan” and still have near-zero capacity to act on it if the plan was adopted and never enforced.

The current capability table already has room for that distinction — the Description of Status, Utilization, and Integration columns exist precisely to capture how a capability is actually used and what it contributes. But across 2,354 rows they are answered 14.4%, 6.6%, and 4.0% of the time, respectively. 82.1% of rows answer none of the three — they exist only as a checked box.

That is not a flaw in these seven counties’ planning — it’s what happens when one table is asked to do two jobs. A capability inventory is built to answer does it exist; a capacity assessment is built to answer can this jurisdiction actually use it. Bolting the second question onto the first, as three optional free-text columns, predictably gets skipped.

Financial capabilities are the largest blind spot. Taxing authority, bonding, fees, and grants make up 23% of all rows in the dataset, but only 3.2% say how they’re used and 1.1% say what mitigation planning gets from them. A county can look financially well-equipped on paper while the record says nothing about whether any of those mechanisms are actually exercised.

Delaware proves the current form can capture capacity signal — its rate is more than four times the statewide average. Niagara and Allegany prove it usually doesn’t: combined, they carry 890 rows, 38% of the entire dataset, with not one utilization or integration note between them.

The Breadth Blind Spot

21 of 169 jurisdictions (12%) show a capability in exactly one of the five domains — 16 of those in Planning & Regulatory only, which for most of them means a single row: adoption of the county Hazard Mitigation Plan.

A capability count of 1 is genuinely ambiguous, and it’s ambiguous in a way the inventory has no way to resolve on its own. It could mean the jurisdiction truly has no other capability worth logging — no staff role, no funding mechanism, no ordinance beyond the plan. Or it could mean the county’s intake process reached that jurisdiction once, for the plan adoption, and never circled back to ask about anything else. Both produce the exact same row count.

That is the structural reason a capability inventory can’t double as a capacity assessment, even a well-completed one. An inventory only speaks when something exists to report; it stays silent everywhere else, and silence doesn’t distinguish “this jurisdiction lacks the capability” from “this jurisdiction was never asked.” See the Capability Inventory report for the full breadth breakdown.

“Hazard Mitigation Plan”

appears on 68 capability rows today

As a Capability Does the plan exist and carry an adoption date?
As a Capacity item Can this jurisdiction actually staff, fund, and enforce it — rated on Availability, Adequacy, and Interest/Need?

Not a duplicate field — a different axis entirely. Both are needed; neither substitutes for the other.

Bridging To The Planned Capacity Assessment

The proposed rapid self-report tool asks jurisdictions directly, item by item, rather than inferring capacity from what a capability row happens to say. ~30 discrete, rateable items across the same five domains this inventory already uses:

Admin & Technical

GIS Coordinator, Floodplain Administrator, Chief Building Official, Emergency Manager, Grant Writer …

Planning & Regulatory

Hazard Mitigation Plan, Comprehensive/Master Plan, Building Code, Zoning & Subdivision Ordinances …

Financial

Tax levy authority, bonding authority, impact/stormwater fees, grant management capacity …

Technical / Infrastructure

GIS/mapping system, HAZUS or risk modeling, asset inventory, warning systems …

Outreach & Coordination

Public education program, CRS participation, mutual aid agreements, citizen/CERT groups …

Each item is rated on three independent scales — Availability (Not Reported / None / Internal / Shared / County-Level / Community Supported), Adequacy (Not Reported / Insufficient / Limited / Adequate / Strong), and Interest/Need (Not Reported / None / Low / Moderate / High) — every time, for every jurisdiction, whether or not a matching capability happens to be checked in this table. That’s what turns the silence documented above into an actual answer.

Recommended path

Pilot the capacity assessment where the capability table is blind

1 · Start with the zeroes

Niagara and Allegany — 890 rows, 38% of the current dataset, currently zero capacity signal of any kind.

2 · Ask everyone the same ~30 items

Regardless of what’s checked in the capability table — so a jurisdiction’s silence stops being ambiguous.

3 · Key it to the same jurisdiction

So a future join can place a capability row next to its capacity rating, closing the loop this report opened.

Net effect: the capability table keeps doing the job it’s good at — a clean inventory of what exists — while the capacity assessment does the job it can’t: telling you, jurisdiction by jurisdiction, whether that existence means anything yet.
