The verdict

Well answered.
Never standardized.

Coverage is not the problem. 16,390 of 18,378 actions — 89.2% — carry a local priority, and among county-plan actions it’s 93.2%. Communities do answer this question. Most of the blank tenth isn’t local reluctance either: 818 of the 1,988 blanks are state-level or unattributed import rows, and two county pools (New York City, 498; Sullivan, 265) account for most of the rest.

The problem is that “Priority” is a free-text box. The source schema defines it as type: "text" with no options — so 16,390 answers arrived in 255 spellings. “High” alone shows up as High, high, HIgh, H, High Priority, High - 1… Meanwhile sibling fields like Action Status are already dropdowns. This column just never got one.

Underneath the mess, everyone is saying the same thing. 85% of filled values are literally High/Medium/Low or a one-letter abbreviation of it, and a mechanical cleanup maps 88.1% of answers onto three buckets. The stubborn residue is per-plan machinery: numeric STAPLEE-style scores (1,332 rows), tier vocabularies, in-plan rankings — each meaningful inside its county’s plan and meaningless beside its neighbors’.

Like duplication and boilerplate, the conventions are a fingerprint of who prepared the annex: Ontario writes letters, Allegany writes rubric scores, Wayne writes “Low (14.5),” Madison ranks every action “High - 1” through “Low - 21.” The fix is a controlled vocabulary that keeps the local signal — without throwing away the plan-specific science.

One Column, Five Instruments

The non-standard values aren’t noise — they’re five different measuring instruments, each internally consistent, each a fingerprint of one plan’s methodology.

Rubric scores · Allegany, Chemung, Delaware, Schuyler, Niagara

Bare numbers — Allegany’s 554 cluster tightly at 11–16, values repeating (125 actions score “12”), so they’re rubric scores, not ranks. Comparable inside the plan; unreadable outside it. Is 15 high? Only the plan knows.

Label + STAPLEE score · Wayne

“Low (14.5),” “Medium (33.5)” — the label and the arithmetic behind it, down to “Low (19.6153).” 150 actions produce 55 distinct values, the most fragmented county in the state. The best data, in the worst format.

Ranked lists · Madison, Chautauqua, Herkimer

“High - 1” through “Low - 21”: a category plus an ordering within it. Madison’s entire annex (149 rows) is written this way. Two facts jammed into one string — each useful, neither queryable.

Tiers · Herkimer, Orange, Albany

“Tier 1 - Top Priority,” “Tier I-B- 13” — 20 spellings across 43 rows. The same idea the new county workflow formalizes properly, appearing here in free-text embryo.

Hedges · Saratoga and others

Saratoga answered “(dependent) M-H” on 48 actions — priority contingent on something the string doesn’t say. Add ranges (“Medium - High”), conditions (“High after flood events”), and split verdicts (“Fish Creek – Low / Mud Creek – High”): honest answers that a single text box forced into ambiguity. A dropdown plus a notes field captures both halves cleanly.

Recommendations

Give locals a real control

The data shows locals want to answer this question — and want to say more than one word. The fix is to make the easy answer structured and give the extra detail somewhere legitimate to live. Four moves, in leverage order:

1 · Make it a dropdown

Change the priority attribute from type: "text" to type: "select" — the same one-attribute config Action Status already uses. Every edit surface becomes a pick-list; the 255-spelling problem can’t recur.

Proposed options

High Medium Low Not Yet Prioritized

Exactly four. 85% of existing answers are already the first three words; every extra option (“Very High,” “Medium-High”) reopens the ambiguity a dropdown exists to close. “Not Yet Prioritized” replaces the 434 N/A-style entries with an intentional state.

2 · Migrate, don’t overwrite

Auto-map the 14,436 clean rows; copy every original string to a priority_original field first so no plan’s language is lost. The 1,954 leftovers become a per-county review queue worked with each plan’s legend — Allegany’s 11–16 rubric converts with two thresholds, Herkimer’s tiers with a five-line lookup. Rows whose owners never answer stay “Not Yet Prioritized” — visible, not silently blank.

3 · Let the plan’s math live somewhere

Wayne’s STAPLEE decimals, Allegany’s rubric points and Madison’s rankings are the best prioritization evidence in the dataset — they just don’t belong in a category label. Add optional companions: priority_score (number), priority_rank (integer), and notes for the hedges (“dependent,” “after flood events”). The label compares across plans; the score keeps its precision within one.

4 · Two lenses: local and county

A second priority field already exists: county_priority, a proper dropdown with five implementation tiers, live in the Sullivan county-workflow pilot (2 rows so far). Keep the two as different lenses — local priority is the jurisdiction’s own judgment; the county tier is the county’s implementation ordering. Don’t merge them; show both. Herkimer and Orange inventing tiers in free text proves the demand was always there.

In the product, the select unlocks what free text never can: priority as an editable pill in every actions table, blanks surfaced per county with a one-click “show un-prioritized” filter, and dashboards that finally answer “how many high-priority flood actions does this county have?” without a 255-branch translation step.
